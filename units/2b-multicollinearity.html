Multicollinearity

When regressors move together, and why the standard errors blow up.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • state the assumption multicollinearity violates, and distinguish the perfect case from the imperfect one;
  • show algebraically why β̂2 comes out 0/0 under perfect multicollinearity;
  • explain why the variances and standard errors become infinite in the same situation;
  • list the consequences of high but imperfect multicollinearity, and say which properties of OLS survive;
  • recognise the classic symptom — a high R2 alongside few significant t-ratios;
  • calculate a variance inflation factor, interpret it, and say what the rule of thumb is worth;
  • choose between the three remedies, and state what each one costs.
Where this sits Unit 2A dealt with getting the model right — which variables belong, and what shape the relationship takes. This unit assumes the model is right and asks a different question: can the sample you happen to have actually estimate it? Multicollinearity is not a mistake in the specification. It is a property of the data, and it is why a perfectly well-specified equation can still come back with nothing significant in it.

1 · Perfect multicollinearity

Perfect multicollinearity is a violation of the classical assumptions — assumption 7 of Unit 1C, which requires that there be no perfect linear relationship between the independent variables. Consider the three-variable regression:

Y = β1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + u

An example of perfect multicollinearity between the two independent variables would be

X3 = α1 + α2X2

where the αs are constants. Read the relationship carefully before deciding whether you have this problem. It is a linear relationship that is forbidden, so X3 = X22 is not multicollinearity at all — which is why the quadratic and cubic specifications of Unit 2A Part 2 are legitimate models rather than broken ones. What is forbidden is one regressor being an exact straight-line function of the others.

2 · The estimator does not exist

From Unit 1D we know that the partial slope in the three-variable model is

β̂2 = [ (Σyx2)(Σx32) − (Σyx3)(Σx2x3) ]  /  [ (Σx22)(Σx32) − (Σx2x3)2 ]

Now assume X3 = α2X2 with α2 ≠ 0. Taking deviations from means, x3 = α2x2, so each of the four sums can be rewritten in terms of X2 alone:

Σyx3 = α2Σyx2  ·  Σx32 = α22Σx22  ·  Σx2x3 = α2Σx22

Substituting all three into the estimator:

β̂2 = [ (Σyx2)(α22Σx22) − (α2Σyx2)(α2Σx22) ]  /  [ (Σx22)(α22Σx22) − (α2Σx22)2 ]  =  0 / 0

Both the numerator and the denominator collapse to exactly zero, because both terms in each bracket are the same quantity. The result is indeterminate — not large, not unstable, but undefined. The same thing happens to β̂3. So the OLS estimators have no solution in the presence of perfect multicollinearity, and this is a matter of arithmetic rather than of insufficient data.

A note on the α subscripts The lecture slides write X3 = α1X2 at this point, using α1 for the slope. Everywhere on this site the subscripts count from the intercept, as Gujarati does, so the intercept of an auxiliary regression is α1 and its first slope is α2. Nothing in the algebra changes; only the label does.

3 · What indeterminacy actually means

Recall what a partial coefficient is meant to say: the expected change in Y when that regressor changes by one unit, holding the other factors constant. Under perfect multicollinearity that instruction cannot be carried out. If X3 = α2X2, then X3 cannot be held constant while X2 moves; the two variables are, in the sample, the same variable measured on a different scale.

The consequence is visible in the fitted values. Writing the fitted equation out and substituting for X3:

Ŷ = β̂1 + β̂2X2 + β̂3X3  ⟹  Ŷ = β̂1 + β̂2X2 + β̂3α2X2  =  β̂1 + (β̂2 + α2β̂3)X2

Only the combination β̂2 + α2β̂3 appears. The data determine that combination perfectly well; nothing whatever determines how it should be divided between the two coefficients. There is not one best-fitting answer but an entire line of them, and every point on that line reproduces the same fitted values, the same residuals and the same residual sum of squares.

4 · The variances are infinite

Consider the same model, with X3 a perfect linear function of X2, and ask what happens to the precision of the estimates rather than to the estimates themselves. Recall from Unit 1D the variance of a partial slope:

var(β̂2) = σ2 / [ Σx22 ( 1 − R232 ) ]

where R232 is the R2 from the regression of X3 on X2. Under perfect multicollinearity that regression fits exactly, so R232 = 1, the bracket is zero, and the variance is infinite. So are the variances and standard errors of β̂2 and β̂3 alike.

Two faces of one failure The point estimate is 0/0 and its variance is infinite. These are not two separate problems. Both are the statement that the sample contains no information at all about how to split the effect between the two regressors — and it is worth noticing that the second formula will still be doing its work, more quietly, when R232 is merely close to one rather than equal to it.

5 · High but imperfect multicollinearity

Perfect multicollinearity is easy to avoid, because you have to put it there yourself. It arises from a mistake in constructing the regressors — including a variable in two units at once, including a set of shares that sum to one alongside a constant, or the dummy variable trap of Unit 3A. Inspect the variables, find the exact relationship, and remove one of them.

When econometricians speak of the problem of multicollinearity, they mean something else: high but imperfect multicollinearity, a linear relationship between two or more independent variables strong enough to affect the estimation of the coefficients materially. Two regressors are imperfectly linearly related when

X3 = α1 + α2X2 + ε   with α2 ≠ 0 and cov(ε, X2) = 0

The stochastic term ε is what distinguishes this from the perfect case: X3 now has some movement of its own that X2 cannot account for, and it is that movement which the regression uses to separate the two effects.

The estimator now exists — but only just

Substituting x3 = α2x2 + ε into the same formula, and using Σεx2 = 0, which is what cov(ε, X2) = 0 gives us:

β̂2 = [ (Σyx2)(α22Σx22 + Σε2) − (α2Σyx2 + Σyε)(α2Σx22) ]  /  [ (Σx22)(α22Σx22 + Σε2) − (α2Σx22)2 ]

The denominator is worth simplifying, because it tells the whole story. Expanding it, the term in α22 cancels and what survives is

(Σx22)(α22Σx22) + (Σx22)(Σε2) − α22(Σx22)2  =  (Σx22)(Σε2)

Everything now rests on Σε2 The whole denominator is the variation in X2 multiplied by the variation in X3 that X2 does not explain. Estimation is possible because Σε2 is not zero. But the smaller ε is — the closer X3 comes to being an exact function of X2 — the smaller that denominator, and the closer we get to the 0/0 of the perfect case. Perfect and imperfect multicollinearity are not two different phenomena. They are the two ends of one continuous scale, and every result in this unit is about where on that scale a given sample sits.

6 · The consequences

Begin with the good news, because it is more surprising than the bad news and it is the part students most often get wrong in examinations.

i The estimates remain unbiased. E(β̂2) = β2 still holds. Multicollinearity is not a specification error, so nothing about it puts the estimator off centre.
ii OLS is still BLUE. No classical assumption has been broken — assumption 7 rules out perfect collinearity only — so the Gauss–Markov theorem still applies and OLS still has the smallest variance in the class of linear unbiased estimators.
iii But that minimum variance can be very large. Being the best of the linear unbiased estimators is no guarantee of being any good. If the information needed to separate two effects is not in the sample, no unbiased estimator can conjure it, and OLS is merely the least bad of a bad set.
A picture you have already seen Two sampling distributions, both centred on the true value, one much wider than the other: that is the whole of consequences (i) to (iii) in one figure, and it is the figure in Unit 2A Part 1 that showed what an irrelevant variable costs. The mechanism is identical — a regressor correlated with X2 inflates the variance of β̂2 by 1/(1 − R232). The difference is only in what we conclude. There, the culprit did not belong in the model and dropping it was free. Here it does belong, and dropping it is not.

What the inflated variance does next

iv The computed t-statistics fall. This follows directly from the formula tk = (β̂k − βH0) / se(β̂k). As the standard error in the denominator rises, the t-ratio is driven towards zero, and we become less likely to reject the null of no effect — for a variable that may matter a great deal.
v Estimates and standard errors become very sensitive to the addition or deletion of a few observations, or to the deletion of a variable. With severe collinearity OLS is forced to lean on small differences between the regressors in order to tell their effects apart, so small changes in the data move those differences a long way.
vi The overall fit is largely unaffected, and so are the coefficients of the regressors that are not collinear. The damage is local. An equation can be badly affected in two of its coefficients and perfectly sound in the rest.
vii A high R2 can sit alongside t-tests that reject nothing. And it is quite possible for the F-test of overall significance to reject the null while not one of the individual t-tests does — a combination which looks like a contradiction and is not.
Why F and t can disagree They are not asking the same question. The F-test asks whether the regressors matter together. Each t-test asks whether one regressor adds anything once the others are already in the equation — and that is precisely the question collinear data cannot answer, because once X3 is in, there is almost nothing left in X2 for the model to use. A joint rejection with no individual ones is the signature of variables that plainly matter as a group but cannot be told apart individually.

7 · Two examples

A student consumption function

COi = β1 + β2Ydi + β3LAi + εi

where COi is the annual consumption expenditure of the ith student on items other than tuition and room and board, Ydi is that student's annual disposable income including gifts, and LAi is their liquid assets — savings and the like. Estimating by OLS:

CÔi =s.e.t = −367.83 + 0.5113 Ydi(1.0307)0.496 + 0.0427 LAi(0.0942)0.453 R̄2 = 0.835

On the t-test both variables are statistically insignificant. Taken at face value, this says that a student's income does not affect their spending — which is not a result anybody would believe. Now re-estimate including disposable income only:

CÔi =s.e.t = −417.45 + 0.9714 Ydi(0.157)6.187 R̄2 = 0.861

Disposable income is now overwhelmingly significant. Three things in that comparison are worth reading off deliberately. The coefficient on income has moved from 0.5113 to 0.9714 — nearly doubling on the deletion of one variable, which is consequence (v). Its standard error has fallen from 1.0307 to 0.157, a reduction by a factor of about six and a half, which is consequence (iv) running in reverse. And the adjusted R2 has risen, from 0.835 to 0.861, on dropping a variable — the sign that liquid assets were contributing nothing that income had not already contributed.

The economics says the same thing as the arithmetic. Disposable income and liquid assets are both measures of how much money a student has available; they are, for this sample, two attempts at one variable. That makes liquid assets a candidate for the second remedy in section 9 rather than a variable whose deletion should worry us.

Demand for petrol, by state

PCONi = β1 + β2UHMi + β3TAXi + β4REGi + εi

with PCONi the petroleum consumption of the ith state in trillions of BTUs, UHMi the urban highway miles within that state, TAXi its gasoline tax in cents per gallon, and REGi its motor vehicle registrations in thousands. Theory gives the expected signs without much argument: more urban highway and more registered vehicles both mean more petrol burned, so β2 and β4 are expected positive, while a tax raises the price at the pump and so β3 is expected negative.

The difficulty is not in the specification, which is entirely sensible, but in the data. Urban highway miles and motor vehicle registrations are both, in large part, measurements of how big a state is. A large state has a great many highway miles and a great many registered vehicles; a small one has few of each. The two regressors will therefore move together closely across the sample, and the equation will struggle to say which of them is doing the work — even though both belong in it, and even though the model as a whole may fit very well.

8 · Detecting multicollinearity

Some collinearity exists in almost every equation ever estimated; the useful question is never whether it is present but how much of it there is. Its severity can also change from sample to sample for the same specification, which already tells you that it is a property of the data rather than of the model. There are no generally accepted statistical tests for it, and this is not an oversight — a test needs a null hypothesis about the population, and this is a condition of the sample. What researchers do instead is develop a feel for its severity by examining a number of characteristics. Three are in common use, and Gujarati and Porter give several more.

1 — A high R2 with few significant t-ratios

This is the classic symptom, and consequence (vii) is the reason for it. If the equation as a whole explains the dependent variable well, and the F-test says the regressors jointly matter, but scarcely any individual coefficient can clear its own t-test, then the sample is telling you that the variables matter but that it cannot apportion the credit.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It tracks the three t-ratios of a correctly specified model as the correlation between two of the regressors is raised, showing the two collinear t-ratios falling below the 5% critical value while R2, F, and the t-ratio of the third regressor are unmoved.

2 — High pairwise correlations

Inspect the correlation coefficients between each pair of regressors. A high pairwise correlation is a sufficient but not a necessary condition for severe multicollinearity: finding one is enough to establish a problem, but failing to find one establishes nothing.

The reason is the major limitation of this method. Groups of variables acting together can produce severe multicollinearity without any single simple correlation coefficient being high. Three regressors may be pairwise unremarkable and yet one of them nearly a linear combination of the other two, and a table of pairwise correlations has no way of seeing that. This is exactly what the third method is built to catch.

3 — The variance inflation factor

The variance inflation factor measures the severity of multicollinearity by asking how much of a given explanatory variable can be explained by all the other explanatory variables in the equation at once. Suppose a model with k independent variables:

Y = β1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + ⋯ + βkXk + ε

A VIF has to be calculated separately for each of the k independent variables.

  1. Run an OLS regression with Xj as a function of all the other explanatory variables in the equation. For X2 that auxiliary regression is

    X2 = α1 + α3X3 + α4X4 + ⋯ + αkXk + u

    Note that Y appears nowhere in it. This diagnostic is entirely about relationships among the regressors.
  2. Collect the R2 from that regression, written Rj2.
  3. Calculate

    VIF(β̂j) = 1 / ( 1 − Rj2 )

The name is earned by where the quantity appears. Rewriting the variance of a slope coefficient:

var(β̂j) = σ2 / [ Σxj2 ( 1 − Rj2 ) ] = ( σ2 / Σxj2 ) · VIF(β̂j)

The first factor is the variance the coefficient would have had if Xj were unrelated to every other regressor. The VIF is the multiple by which collinearity inflates it, and so it gives the speed with which the variances — and by extension the standard errors — increase as collinearity worsens.

Reading a VIF As Rj2 → 1, VIF → ∞. The higher the VIF, the more severe the effects of multicollinearity.
There are no formal critical values. The common rule of thumb is that a VIF above 10 indicates severe multicollinearity, which happens when Rj2 exceeds 0.9; some books use 5 instead. Neither number comes from a distribution.
Two cautions go with it. It is possible to have large multicollinearity effects without a large VIF — a coefficient with a small Σxj2 can be imprecise at a VIF of 3. And the number worth quoting alongside the VIF is √VIF, because that, not the VIF itself, is the factor by which the standard error is multiplied and therefore the factor by which the t-ratio is divided. A VIF of 10 sounds alarming and means the standard error has roughly tripled.

9 · Remedies

Remedy 1 — do nothing

This is a respectable answer, and often the right one. The coefficients may still be significant and may still meet expectations, in which case there is nothing to fix. More importantly, if you delete a multicollinear variable that belongs in the model, you have caused specification bias — you have traded a variance problem, which leaves the estimator centred on the truth, for a bias problem, which does not. Unit 2A Part 1 set out the cost of an omitted relevant variable in full, and none of it is undone by the fact that the omission was made for a good reason.

There is a second argument for restraint. Every time a regression is rerun on the same sample, we risk arriving at a specification that works by accident on that sample — the data mining the general-to-specific approach of Unit 2A Part 1 warns against. A model reshaped until its t-ratios look respectable has t-ratios that no longer mean what they claim to mean.

Why dropping a variable is harmless in one case and not the other Under perfect multicollinearity, X3 carries no information at all that X2 does not already carry: it is X2 on a different scale. Dropping it loses nothing — the fitted values are identical, and section 3 showed why. Under imperfect multicollinearity that is no longer true. X3 has its own variation ε, and if X3 genuinely belongs in the model, that variation belongs in the explanation. Dropping it now omits a relevant variable and biases everything correlated with it. The very ε that made estimation possible is what makes deletion costly.

Remedy 2 — drop a redundant variable

Two or more variables in an equation that measure essentially the same thing are redundant, and the previous warning does not apply to them. Dropping a redundant variable is not creating a specification error; it is making up for one, since including two measures of a single concept is the overfitting of Unit 2A Part 1.

The distinction is a matter of economics rather than statistics, and no diagnostic will make it for you. A high correlation between two regressors can arise either from two measures of one concept or from two genuinely distinct forces that happen to move together. Disposable income and liquid assets in section 7 are plausibly the former; urban highway miles and vehicle registrations are plausibly the latter, since a policy could raise one without raising the other. Only the first pair should be reduced to one variable.

Remedy 3 — increase the size of the sample

A larger sample will normally reduce the variance of the estimated coefficients, and so diminish the impact of multicollinearity. The reason is visible in the variance formula: adding observations increases Σxj2, which sits in the denominator, and this happens whatever the value of Rj2. More data does not make the regressors less correlated. It buys enough extra variation that the correlation matters less.

The same formula explains a related complaint. Too small a sample — one where the number of observations barely exceeds the number of parameters to be estimated — produces very similar consequences to multicollinearity, for the same reason and with the same symptoms. The econometrician Arthur Goldberger, reacting to the profession's preoccupation with multicollinearity, coined the term micronumerosity for it: the problem of small sample size, dressed up in language grand enough to be taken as seriously as its famous cousin. The joke has a point. Both conditions are shortages of information in the sample, and neither is a flaw in the model.

Unfortunately, while it is a useful alternative to consider, collecting more data is not always possible. There are only so many states, so many years of quarterly national accounts, so many countries in the region.

The honest summary Multicollinearity is a data problem, not a model problem. The specification can be perfect and the theory impeccable, and the sample can still be unable to separate two effects that move together in it. That is why "do nothing" is a serious answer, why the only real cure is different or better data, and why the worst response is to keep deleting variables until the output looks convincing. Report the imprecision; do not estimate it away.

10 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

For the model Y = β1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + u, suppose X3 = α2X2 exactly. Show that β̂2 is indeterminate, and explain in words what the algebra is telling you about the sample.
Show solution

The substitutions. In deviation form x3 = α2x2, so Σyx3 = α2Σyx2, Σx32 = α22Σx22 and Σx2x3 = α2Σx22.

The numerator. (Σyx2)(α22Σx22) − (α2Σyx2)(α2Σx22). Both terms are α22(Σyx2)(Σx22), so it is zero.

The denominator. (Σx22)(α22Σx22) − (α2Σx22)2 = α22(Σx22)2 − α22(Σx22)2 = 0. So β̂2 = 0/0, and by the same steps so is β̂3.

In words. Not "the estimate is very large" or "the estimate is unstable" — it does not exist. The fitted equation can be rewritten Ŷ = β̂1 + (β̂2 + α2β̂3)X2, so the sample identifies the combination β̂2 + α2β̂3 and nothing else. Infinitely many pairs give that same combination, and every one of them produces identical fitted values and an identical RSS. Least squares has no basis for preferring any of them.

Equivalently, and this is worth being able to say too: the interpretation of a partial coefficient requires holding the other regressors constant, and X3 cannot be held constant while X2 moves.

A model Y = β1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + β4X4 + u is estimated on 30 observations. The auxiliary regressions give R22 = 0.94, R32 = 0.92 and R42 = 0.35. Compute the three VIFs, apply the rule of thumb, and state precisely what the largest of them means for the standard error.
Show solution

The calculations. VIF = 1/(1 − Rj2):

VIF2 = 1/0.06 = 16.67  ·  VIF3 = 1/0.08 = 12.50  ·  VIF4 = 1/0.65 = 1.54

The rule of thumb. Both X2 and X3 exceed 10, so both are flagged as severely collinear with the rest of the equation. X4 at 1.54 is unremarkable, and its coefficient should be estimated about as precisely as if the others were not there — consequence (vi), that the damage is local.

What 16.67 means. The variance of β̂2 is 16.67 times what it would have been had X2 been uncorrelated with X3 and X4. The quantity to report is the square root, since standard errors and not variances are what appear in a t-ratio: √16.67 = 4.08, so the standard error is about four times larger and the t-ratio about four times smaller than it would otherwise have been. A coefficient that would have had t = 6 is reduced to t = 1.5, and would be reported as insignificant.

Note what the exercise does not establish. None of these numbers says X2 should be dropped. They say the sample cannot estimate its coefficient precisely, which is a different and more honest claim.

An equation with three regressors, estimated on 30 observations, returns R2 = 0.94 with no individual t-ratio above 1.5. Compute the F-statistic for overall significance and test it at 5%, given that the critical value F326 is 2.98. Reconcile the two results, and say what you would look at next.
Show solution

The F-statistic. With k = 4 parameters including the intercept, the numerator has k − 1 = 3 degrees of freedom and the denominator n − k = 26:

F = [ R2 / (k − 1) ] / [ (1 − R2) / (n − k) ] = (0.94 / 3) / (0.06 / 26) = 0.3133 / 0.002308 = 135.8

Since 135.8 ≫ 2.98, reject the null that all the slope coefficients are jointly zero, and by a very wide margin.

Reconciling them. There is no contradiction, because the two tests ask different questions. F asks whether the regressors matter together, and the answer is emphatically yes. Each t asks whether one regressor adds anything given that the others are already in the equation, and with collinear data the answer is no, because by then there is almost nothing left in that regressor that the others have not already supplied. This combination — a high R2, a decisive F, and no significant t — is the classic symptom of severe multicollinearity.

What next. Compute the VIFs, which is the diagnostic that identifies which regressors are the collinear ones; pairwise correlations as a supplement, remembering that they can miss a variable that is collinear with a combination of two others. Then decide on economic grounds whether any of the flagged variables are redundant measures of one concept, or whether all of them genuinely belong — because that, and not the size of the VIF, determines whether dropping one is a repair or a new error.

"Multicollinearity biases the coefficients, so a model suffering from it should not be trusted." Assess this statement. In your answer state which properties of OLS survive high multicollinearity and which do not, and explain why the answer would be different if the collinearity were perfect.
Show solution

The statement is wrong on the first clause. High but imperfect multicollinearity does not bias anything: E(β̂j) = βj still holds. No classical assumption has been violated — assumption 7 forbids only perfect collinearity — so the Gauss–Markov theorem still applies and OLS remains BLUE. Unbiasedness and minimum variance in the class of linear unbiased estimators both survive.

What does not survive is usefulness. Being minimum variance is a statement about the competition, not about the size of the variance. The variances, covariances and standard errors of the collinear coefficients can be enormous, so t-ratios collapse, confidence intervals become uninformatively wide, and the estimates jump about when a few observations or a variable are added or removed. A coefficient can be unbiased and still be useless for any decision.

So is the conclusion right? Partly, and for the wrong reason. The model should not be trusted for statements about the individual collinear coefficients. It can still be trusted for the overall fit, for prediction within the range of the data — where the regressors will go on moving together as they did in the sample — and for the coefficients of the regressors that are not collinear, which are unaffected.

If the collinearity were perfect, none of this analysis would apply, because there would be no estimates to have properties. OLS returns 0/0: the coefficients are not biased, not imprecise, but undefined, and their variances are infinite. That is assumption 7 actually failing, rather than being approached.

A researcher's wage equation includes both years of schooling and years of schooling measured in months, and finds the software refuses to estimate it. A colleague's equation includes both parental income and parental education, and estimates fine but returns two insignificant coefficients with a VIF of 12 on each. Diagnose both, and recommend a course of action for each with reasons.
Show solution

The first is perfect multicollinearity. Months of schooling is exactly 12 times years of schooling, so one regressor is an exact linear function of the other, assumption 7 fails, and the cross-product matrix cannot be inverted. The software is not malfunctioning; there is genuinely no unique answer for it to return. The remedy is free and non-negotiable: drop one of the two. Nothing whatever is lost, because the retained variable contains everything the deleted one contained, and the fitted values are identical either way.

The second is high but imperfect multicollinearity. Parental income and parental education are correlated — better-educated parents tend to earn more — but neither is a function of the other, so estimation succeeds and the estimates remain unbiased. A VIF of 12 means each standard error is √12 ≈ 3.5 times what it would have been otherwise, which is easily enough to push a real effect below the 5% bar.

What to recommend. Not deletion. These are two distinct economic forces, not two measures of one thing: a policy could raise parental education without raising parental income, and the two have separate theoretical channels to a child's wage. Dropping either one omits a relevant variable and biases the coefficient on the other, which is a worse error than the one being fixed — a variance problem exchanged for a bias problem. The defensible options are to report the results as they stand while saying plainly that the sample cannot separate the two effects, to note that they are jointly significant by an F-test even though neither is individually so, and, if it is at all possible, to enlarge the sample.

The contrast between the two cases is the whole of section 9: what makes deletion safe is not the strength of the correlation but whether the deleted variable belongs in the model.
