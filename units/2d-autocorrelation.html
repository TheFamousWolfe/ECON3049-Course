Autocorrelation

Errors that remember the last period — Durbin–Watson, Breusch–Godfrey and the remedies.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • state the assumption autocorrelation violates, and write the AR(1) error process;
  • derive var(ut) and cov(ut, ut−s) under AR(1), and say what stationarity is doing in that derivation;
  • list the consequences for OLS, and explain why the reported standard error understates;
  • recognise positive and negative autocorrelation in a residual plot;
  • carry out a Durbin–Watson test, apply the decision rules, and state its four limitations;
  • carry out a Breusch–Godfrey test and say what it can do that Durbin–Watson cannot;
  • distinguish pure from impure autocorrelation, and choose between Newey–West and GLS.
The last of the relaxed assumptions Unit 2C broke assumption 5 — the disturbances no longer had a common variance. This unit breaks assumption 6: the disturbances are no longer independent of one another. The two failures rhyme. In both, OLS stays unbiased and loses efficiency, and in both the damage lands on the standard errors rather than the coefficients. What differs is the size of the damage: as section 5 shows, autocorrelation with a persistent regressor is considerably the more dangerous of the two.

1 · The no-autocorrelation assumption

One of the classical assumptions is that there is no autocorrelation between the disturbances:

cov(ui, uj | X) = cov(ui, uj) = E(uiuj) = 0   for i ≠ j

The assumption says that the error term associated with any one observation is not influenced by, or correlated with, the disturbance term of any other observation. When it is violated we have autocorrelation, and cov(ui, uj) ≠ 0.

Time series data present the most common example of correlated errors. Put the assumption in a time series context: if there is some external shock to a model in one period — a hurricane, a change of government, an oil price spike — then no autocorrelation implies that this disturbance affects that period only. It will not be carried over into another period:

cov(ut, ut+s) = 0   for s ≠ 0

where ut is the error in the current period and ut+s the error s periods ahead; the same statement is sometimes written with ut−s instead. Stated that way the assumption is obviously heroic. Very little that happens to an economy is finished within one quarter. Autocorrelation can also be found in cross-sectional data, where it is called spatial autocorrelation — neighbouring districts share whatever the model has left out — but our focus is the time series case.

2 · Time series data, and why it is harder

Time series data involve a single entity — a person, a country, a corporation, a state — over multiple points in time, and are used to analyse questions that cross-sectional data cannot touch. The notation changes accordingly:

cross-section: Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui,   i = 1 … N

time series: Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ut,   t = 1 … T

Dealing with time series is more difficult than dealing with cross-sections, for four reasons that are worth having in mind for the whole unit.

i The order of the observations is fixed. A cross-section can be shuffled without loss; a time series cannot, because the ordering carries information.
ii Samples tend to be much smaller. Forty years of quarterly data is 160 observations, and no amount of effort will produce more of them this year.
iii The underlying theory can be quite complex — a good deal of it belongs to Forecasting and Econometrics II rather than here.
iv The disturbance is often affected by events of a previous period. Which means we often have autocorrelation even when the model is correctly specified — the situation section 3 calls pure.

3 · First-order autocorrelation

Pure autocorrelation occurs when the classical assumption of no autocorrelation is violated in a correctly specified equation. Its most popular form, and the only one this unit works through in full, is first-order autocorrelation:

Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ut

ut = ρ ut−1 + εt

Here ρ is the first-order autocorrelation coefficient, or the coefficient of autocorrelation at lag 1. The only violation is autocorrelation itself: εt is assumed to have zero mean and constant variance and to be independent over time, and all explanatory variables are independent of all error terms. Written out, the assumptions in force are

E(ut | X) = E(u) = 0  ·  var(ut | X) = σ2  ·  cov(ut, ut−1) ≠ 0

E(εt | ut−1) = E(εt) = 0  ·  cov(εt, εt−1) = 0  ·  var(εt) = σε2

Reading ρ The magnitude of ρ indicates the strength of the autocorrelation, and ρ = 0 means there is none.
If ρ ≠ 0 we require −1 < ρ < 1, that is |ρ| < 1. When that holds the first-order autoregressive process is stationary: the mean, variance and covariance of ut do not change over time. Section 5 cannot be done without this — it is the step that lets us solve for a single σ2.
ρ > 0 is positive autocorrelation; ρ < 0 is negative.

Autocorrelation takes other forms. Second-order autocorrelation carries two lags of the error term, third-order three, and so on:

ut = ρ1ut−1 + ρ2ut−2 + εt

ut = ρ1ut−1 + ρ2ut−2 + ρ3ut−3 + εt

4 · The consequences

i No bias in the coefficient estimates. Unbiasedness does not hinge on the assumption of no autocorrelation, so it survives its failure intact.
ii We lose the "B" in BLUE. OLS is no longer the minimum-variance estimator of all the linear unbiased estimators, because the Gauss–Markov theorem needs assumption 6 and no longer has it.
iii The OLS estimates of the standard errors are biased, which makes hypothesis tests unreliable — and unreliable even asymptotically, so this is not a defect that a larger sample repairs.

The last two can both be seen by computing the variance of the OLS estimator in a model where the only assumption violated is that of autocorrelation, which is what section 5 does.

5 · The variance of the OLS estimator under AR(1)

Take the simple regression with first-order autocorrelated errors and assume |ρ| < 1. Start with the mean of the disturbance:

E(ut) = ρE(ut−1) + E(εt) = 0

Now the variance:

var(ut) = var(ρut−1 + εt) = var(ρut−1) + var(εt) = ρ2var(ut−1) + σε2

Why does the covariance term vanish? The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus twice the covariance. Here that covariance is cov(ρut−1, εt), which is zero because εt is independent of everything dated t − 1 and earlier — it is this period's fresh shock. That is exactly what E(εt | ut−1) = E(εt) = 0 was assumed for.

Under stationarity var(ut) = var(ut−1) = σ2, so the equation becomes σ2 = ρ2σ2 + σε2, which solves to

var(ut) = σ2 = σε2 / ( 1 − ρ2 )

Notice what that says: the disturbance is more variable than the shock that drives it, and increasingly so as |ρ| approaches one, because each period inherits a share of every shock that came before. Next the covariance:

cov(ut, ut−1) = E(utut−1) = E([ρut−1 + εt]ut−1) = ρE(ut−12) + E(εtut−1)

The second term is zero, for the same reason as before — εt is uncorrelated with ut−1 — so

cov(ut, ut−1) = ρE(ut−12) = ρ [ σε2 / ( 1 − ρ2 ) ]

and generally, s periods apart,

cov(ut, ut−s) = ρs [ σε2 / ( 1 − ρ2 ) ]  ·  corr(ut, ut−s) = ρs

The correlation result is the memorable one. The influence of a shock decays geometrically: with ρ = 0.8 a disturbance is still 64% correlated with the one two periods later and 33% with the one five periods later. Nothing about this process is finished quickly.

What this does to var(β̂2)

If we ignore the autocorrelation, the reported variance of the slope is the familiar

var(β̂2) = σ2 / Σxt2

whereas the truth, when the errors follow AR(1), is

var(β̂2)AR1 = ( σ2 / Σxt2 ) · [ 1 + 2ρ ( Σxtxt−1 / Σxt2 ) + 2ρ2 ( Σxtxt−2 / Σxt2 ) + ⋯ ]

Read the bracket The first factor is exactly the conventional formula, so everything the autocorrelation does is in the bracket.
Set ρ = 0 and every term after the 1 disappears, returning the familiar result — the conventional formula is again a special case rather than a different one.
With ρ > 0 and a positively autocorrelated X — which is the normal situation, since economic time series are persistent — every term in the bracket is positive, the bracket exceeds 1, and the conventional standard error understates the truth. t-ratios are then too large and tests reject far too often.
Note also what the bracket depends on: not only ρ but the serial correlation of the regressor. Autocorrelated errors alongside a regressor that jumps about at random do much less damage than the same errors alongside a trending one.

6 · Impure autocorrelation

Impure autocorrelation occurs when the assumption of no autocorrelation is violated in an incorrectly specified equation. Incorrect functional forms, omitted variables and an inadequate dynamic specification of the model all lead to findings of autocorrelation. This explains why tests for autocorrelation are very often used as misspecification tests — a significant Durbin–Watson statistic is at least as likely to be telling you the model is wrong as telling you the disturbance has a memory.

A missing variable

Suppose you are using monthly data to estimate a model explaining the demand for ice cream in the UK. The weather will obviously be an important factor, but suppose ice cream consumption I is modelled as a function of price P and income Y alone:

It = β1 + β2Pt + β3Yt + ut

The impact of weather is then hidden in the error term, ut = β4Wt + εt, and we are thus likely to find a pattern in the residuals. The reason is that weather is itself autocorrelated: a warm July is followed by a warm August. The disturbance inherits the serial correlation of the variable that was left out of it, which is the whole mechanism of impure autocorrelation in one example.

The wrong functional form

Suppose the true equation is quadratic but a linear one is estimated:

true: Yt = β1 + β2Xt + β3Xt2 + u

run: Yt = a1 + a2Xt + v

The new error term v is a function of the true error term and of the differences between the linear and the polynomial functional forms. Those differences are systematic — the straight line is above the curve in the middle of the range and below it at both ends — and such differences often follow an autoregressive pattern. Again the test will fire, and again the remedy is to fix the model rather than the standard errors.

7 · Detecting it: the informal method

The informal method is graphical analysis of the residuals. We do not observe u, so we use û as a proxy, exactly as in Unit 2C. Return to the ice cream example, this time including a proxy for weather — temperature:

It = β1 + β2Pt + β3Yt + β4Wt + ut

Estimate the regression, collect the OLS residuals ût = It − Ît, and then look at them two ways. Plot the residuals over time — a time sequence plot — and look for patterns. And produce a scatter plot with the current residuals ût on the vertical axis and the lagged residuals ût−1 on the horizontal. 8 · The Durbin–Watson test

The classic test for autocorrelation, due to James Durbin and Geoffrey Watson, and used to detect first-order — AR(1) — autocorrelation. The statistic is based on the OLS residuals:

DW = Σ ( ût − ût−1 )2 / Σ ût2

Simple algebra shows that

DW ≈ 2 ( 1 − ρ̂ )

where ρ̂ is the correlation between ût and ût−1. Everything about reading the statistic follows from that one relation:

The scale
ρ̂ close to 0DW close to 2no autocorrelation
ρ̂ > 0DW smaller than 2positive autocorrelation
ρ̂ < 0DW larger than 2negative autocorrelation
ρ̂ close to 1DW close to 0strong positive
ρ̂ close to −1DW close to 4strong negative
Taken together, DW ranges from 0 to 4, with values close to 2 indicating no autocorrelation, close to 0 positive, and close to 4 negative.

The test statistic must be compared with two sets of critical values, based on the number of explanatory variables k and on the sample size, usually labelled dU for upper and dL for lower. Unlike the F, χ2 and t tests met so far, the boundary between reject and do-not-reject is not clear-cut: there is an inconclusive region.

The decision rules
Null hypothesisDecisionIf
No positive autocorrelationReject0 < d < dL
No positive autocorrelationNo decisiondL ≤ d ≤ dU
No negative autocorrelationReject4 − dL < d < 4
No negative autocorrelationNo decision4 − dU ≤ d ≤ 4 − dL
No autocorrelation, either signDo not rejectdU < d < 4 − dU

Four limitations i. It can only test for first-order autocorrelation.
ii. The regression must not include lagged values of the dependent variable among the explanatory variables — that is, nothing of the form Yt = β1 + β2Xt + β3Yt−1 + ut. Such autoregressive models are studied in Forecasting and Econometrics II, and they are extremely common, which is a large hole in the test's coverage.
iii. The regression must include an intercept.
iv. It assumes the error terms are normally distributed.
To which the figure adds a fifth in practice: the inconclusive region is wide enough that a real result often lands in it.

9 · The Breusch–Godfrey test

To avoid some of those pitfalls, Breusch and Godfrey developed a general test of autocorrelation. It allows lagged values of the dependent variable, and it allows higher-order autocorrelation — it can be used to test first order, second order, third order and so on. It works by analysing how well the lagged residuals explain the residual of the original equation, in an equation that also includes all the original explanatory variables. If the lagged residuals are statistically significant, the null of no serial correlation is rejected.

Consider the model and an assumed qth-order error process:

Yt = β1 + β2X2t + β3X3t + ⋯ + βkXkt + ut

ut = ρ1ut−1 + ρ2ut−2 + ⋯ + ρqut−q + εt

If ρ1 = ρ2 = ⋯ = ρq = 0 there is no autocorrelation, and that is the null hypothesis.

  1. Estimate the model and collect the residuals ût.
  2. Run the auxiliary regression of ût on X2t, X3t, … Xkt, ût−1, ût−2, … ût−q. The original regressors stay in. Leaving them out would attribute to the lagged residuals whatever the regressors could have explained.
  3. Obtain the R2 from the auxiliary regression. The test statistic is

    (n − q)R2  ~  χ2(q)

    with q degrees of freedom — one for each lag being tested.

Example 1 — the ice cream equation, tested at second order

With the weather proxy included, the estimated equation on 30 monthly observations is

Ît = 0.1973 − 1.044 P + 0.0003 Y + 0.0035 W  ·  n = 30

Collecting the residuals and running the auxiliary regression with two lags:

û = 0.048 − 0.165 P + 0.000 Y + 0.000 W + 0.468 ût−1 − 0.128 ût−2

n = 30  ·  R2 = 0.1496

(n − q)R2 = (30 − 2)(0.1496) = 4.188

The critical value at α = 0.05 with two degrees of freedom is χ2(2) = 5.99. Since 4.188 is below it, we cannot reject the null of no autocorrelation. Note the coefficient on the first lag, 0.468 — not a small number — and yet the joint test does not reject. With n = 30 there is simply not enough information to be confident, which is limitation (ii) of section 2 arriving in practice.

Example 2 — a consumption function, tested at third order

ln Ct = β1 + β2 ln Yt + ut

Estimating on 46 observations gives ln Ct = 0.1973 + 1.044 ln Yt, and the auxiliary regression with three lags is

û = −0.017 + 0.001 ln Yt + 0.768 ût−1 + 0.328 ût−2 − 0.305 ût−3

n = 46  ·  R2 = 0.7069

(n − q)R2 = (46 − 3)(0.7069) = 30.397

Against χ2(3) = 7.82 at 5%, we reject the null: there is autocorrelation. An auxiliary R2 of 0.71 means the lagged residuals explain seven-tenths of the variation in the current one, which is not a subtle finding. A consumption function in logs on annual data is exactly where one would expect it, too — consumption is smooth, and whatever the model omits persists.

The drawback: how do we know q? We cannot know the order of autocorrelation a priori and have to experiment to find it. In practice one uses correlograms, which belong to Forecasting and Econometrics II, or runs the auxiliary regression at various values of q and chooses the lag length by AIC or SC, both of which were met in Unit 2A Part 1. In this course you will be told which order to test for.

10 · Remedies

In many cases the finding of autocorrelation is an indication that the model is misspecified. So the first task is the same as it was in Unit 2C: try to find out whether the autocorrelation is pure, and not due to specification bias. Section 6 gives the two things to look for — a missing variable that is itself persistent, and a functional form that does not fit. Only if the autocorrelation is pure should the following remedies be considered.

Remedy 1 — Newey–West standard errors

Recall that OLS is unbiased, though less precise, in the presence of autocorrelation. So instead of re-estimating anything, we can keep OLS and correct only the standard errors, by a procedure developed by Newey and West. It is an extension of White's heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors from Unit 2C, and the corrected figures are known as HAC — heteroscedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent — standard errors, or simply Newey–West standard errors.

+ The benefit. The HAC method handles both problems at once, so an equation suffering from Unit 2C's complaint and this one needs only a single correction. Most modern packages will compute them on request.
− The drawback. Newey–West standard errors tend to perform poorly when the sample size is small — and time series samples, as section 2 noted, are usually small. The correction requires a judgement about how many lags of the residual to allow for, and in a short, strongly autocorrelated sample it narrows the gap in section 5 substantially without closing it.

Remedy 2 — generalised least squares

GLS rids an equation of pure first-order serial correlation and restores the minimum variance property to its estimation. It does this by transforming the equation into one that does not have the problem. Start from the simple regression with AR(1) errors and substitute the error process in:

Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ut  ⟹  Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ρut−1 + εt

Lag the original model by one period and solve it for ut−1:

Yt−1 = β1 + β2Xt−1 + ut−1  ⟹  ut−1 = Yt−1 − β1 − β2Xt−1

Substituting that back in:

Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ρ( Yt−1 − β1 − β2Xt−1 ) + εt

and rearranging so that everything in Y is on the left:

Yt − ρYt−1 = β1 − ρβ1 + β2Xt − ρβ2Xt−1 + εt

Yt − ρYt−1 = β1(1 − ρ) + β2( Xt − ρXt−1 ) + εt

which we can write as

Yt* = β1* + β2Xt* + εt   where Yt* = Yt − ρYt−1,   β1* = β1(1 − ρ),   Xt* = Xt − ρXt−1

Two things to notice The error term is no longer serially correlated. It is εt, which was assumed independent over time from the start — the transformation has stripped out exactly the part of ut that was inherited from last period.
The slope is the same as in the original regression. β2 appears untransformed, so what we estimate from the starred equation is the coefficient we actually wanted. Only the intercept is rescaled, by (1 − ρ), and is easily recovered.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It shows the residuals of a regression whose errors follow an AR(1) process with ρ = 0.8, and the residuals of the same sample after the transformation Yt − ρYt−1, with ρ̂, the Durbin–Watson statistic and the estimated slope reported for each.

The problem — what is ρ?

Unfortunately we do not know the value of ρ, so we have to estimate it. This can be done from the residuals, by regressing ût on ût−1:

ût = ρ ût−1 + vt

But since we then use ρ̂ instead of the true ρ, this is feasible GLS — FGLS, or estimated GLS — the same idea met in Unit 2C, and it carries the same warning: the properties are large-sample ones, because the weights were estimated rather than known.

Most practitioners iterate. Having obtained the FGLS estimates, compute a new value of ρ̂ from the new residuals and redo the FGLS, continuing until the estimate of ρ̂ stops changing. The two popular iterative procedures under autocorrelation are the Cochrane–Orcutt procedure and the Prais–Winsten procedure. The difference between them is what happens to the first observation: the transformation Yt − ρYt−1 cannot be applied at t = 1, since there is no Y0. Cochrane–Orcutt simply drops it; Prais–Winsten keeps it, rescaled by √(1 − ρ2). With T = 160 the difference is negligible; with T = 25 it is not.

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

A regression on 40 quarterly observations with two explanatory variables gives Σ(ût − ût−1)2 = 38.4 and Σût2 = 48.0. Compute the Durbin–Watson statistic, infer ρ̂, and reach a decision at 5% given dL = 1.39 and dU = 1.60. What would you have concluded had the statistic been 1.50?
Show solution

The statistic.

DW = 38.4 / 48.0 = 0.80

Inferring ρ̂. From DW ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂), rearranging gives ρ̂ ≈ 1 − DW/2 = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60. Substantial positive autocorrelation.

The decision. DW = 0.80 falls in the region 0 < d < dL = 1.39, so we reject the null of no positive autocorrelation. There is evidence of positive first-order autocorrelation.

Had DW been 1.50. That lies between dL = 1.39 and dU = 1.60, which is the inconclusive region: the test returns no verdict at all. This is not the same as failing to reject — one may not report "no evidence of autocorrelation" from an inconclusive result. The sensible response is to run a Breusch–Godfrey test, which has an ordinary rejection region and no such zone.

A model with three explanatory variables is estimated on 50 quarterly observations. A Breusch–Godfrey test at second order gives an auxiliary R2 of 0.214. Carry out the test at 5%, given χ2(2) = 5.99. Then state two things this test can do that a Durbin–Watson test cannot, and one thing it requires of you that Durbin–Watson does not.
Show solution

The test. With q = 2 lags on 50 observations:

(n − q)R2 = (50 − 2)(0.214) = 48 × 0.214 = 10.27

Against χ2(2) = 5.99 in the right tail, 10.27 > 5.99, so reject the null of no autocorrelation at 5%.

Two things it can do. First, it tests higher-order autocorrelation — here second order, and it extends to any q — whereas Durbin–Watson only ever tests the first. Second, it remains valid when the equation contains lagged values of the dependent variable, which invalidate Durbin–Watson entirely and which appear in a great many time series models.

One might add a third: it returns a straightforward reject or do-not-reject, with no inconclusive region to fall into.

What it requires of you. A choice of q. Durbin–Watson has no such parameter, while Breusch–Godfrey cannot be run until you decide how many lags to include, and that cannot be known a priori. In practice one uses a correlogram, or compares lag lengths by AIC or SC — and in this course you will be told which order to test.

Starting from Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ut with ut = ρut−1 + εt, derive var(ut) and cov(ut, ut−1). State clearly where stationarity is used and where the independence of εt is used.
Show solution

The variance. Taking variances of both sides of the error process:

var(ut) = var(ρut−1 + εt) = ρ2var(ut−1) + σε2 + 2ρ·cov(ut−1, εt)

The covariance term is zero because εt is independent of everything dated t − 1 and earlier — this is the first use of the independence assumption. That leaves var(ut) = ρ2var(ut−1) + σε2.

Now stationarity is used: it says var(ut) = var(ut−1) = σ2, one number rather than a sequence. Without it the equation relates two different unknowns and cannot be solved. With it:

σ2 = ρ2σ2 + σε2  ⟹  σ2(1 − ρ2) = σε2  ⟹  σ2 = σε2 / (1 − ρ2)

Note this also shows why |ρ| < 1 is required: at |ρ| = 1 the denominator is zero and the variance is not finite, so the process is not stationary and none of this applies.

The covariance.

cov(ut, ut−1) = E(utut−1) = E([ρut−1 + εt]ut−1) = ρE(ut−12) + E(εtut−1)

The second term is zero — the second use of the independence of εt. And E(ut−12) is just the variance we have already found, since E(u) = 0. So

cov(ut, ut−1) = ρσε2/(1 − ρ2),   and generally cov(ut, ut−s) = ρsσε2/(1 − ρ2)

Dividing the covariance by the variance gives the tidiest form of the result: corr(ut, ut−s) = ρs. The memory decays geometrically but never quite vanishes.

"Autocorrelation is much like heteroscedasticity — OLS stays unbiased and the standard errors need fixing." Assess. In your answer explain why the reported standard error is biased and in which direction, and say what role the regressor's own behaviour plays in the size of the problem.
Show solution

The statement is right as far as it goes. Both are failures of a classical assumption that leave E(β̂2) = β2 intact, because unbiasedness depends on assumptions 3 and 4 rather than on 5 or 6. Both cost the "B" in BLUE, so OLS is no longer minimum variance. And in both the reported standard error is computed from a formula that no longer applies.

Why the standard error is biased. The reported figure is √(σ̂2/Σxt2), which is the correct variance only when the disturbances are uncorrelated. Under AR(1) the truth carries an extra bracket:

var(β̂2)AR1 = (σ2/Σxt2) [1 + 2ρ(Σxtxt−1/Σxt2) + 2ρ2(Σxtxt−2/Σxt2) + ⋯]

The direction. When ρ > 0 and X is itself positively autocorrelated, every term in the bracket is positive, the bracket exceeds one, and the reported standard error is too small. t-ratios are inflated, and a nominal 5% test rejects far more often than 5% of the time — the dangerous direction, because it manufactures significance rather than hiding it.

The role of the regressor. This is what the statement misses. The bracket depends not only on ρ but on Σxtxt−s/Σxt2 — the regressor's own serial correlation. Autocorrelated errors alongside a regressor that jumps about at random do relatively little harm; the same errors alongside a trending or persistent regressor, which is the normal case in economics, do a great deal. That is why autocorrelation is the more dangerous of the two problems: heteroscedasticity in Unit 2C took the reported standard error down to roughly three-quarters of the truth, while autocorrelation with a persistent regressor can take it below a third.

One further difference. The statement's implied remedy — "fix the standard errors" — is weaker here. White's correction works well in moderate samples; Newey–West performs poorly in the small samples that time series work usually provides. And a finding of autocorrelation is more often a symptom of misspecification than a finding of heteroscedasticity is, which is why these tests double as misspecification tests.

Derive the GLS transformation for a model with AR(1) errors. Explain why the transformed error is not serially correlated, why the slope coefficient is unaffected, and what practical difficulty stands in the way of actually using it.
Show solution

The derivation. Substituting ut = ρut−1 + εt into Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ut, and replacing ut−1 by Yt−1 − β1 − β2Xt−1 from the lagged model:

Yt = β1 + β2Xt + ρ(Yt−1 − β1 − β2Xt−1) + εt

Collecting the Y terms on the left and factorising:

Yt − ρYt−1 = β1(1 − ρ) + β2(Xt − ρXt−1) + εt

which is Y* = β1* + β2X* + εt with Y* = Yt − ρYt−1, X* = Xt − ρXt−1 and β1* = β1(1 − ρ).

Why the error is clean. The transformed disturbance is εt itself, which was assumed from the outset to have zero mean, constant variance and no serial correlation. The ρ-differencing has removed precisely the part of ut that was carried over from the previous period, leaving only this period's fresh shock.

Why the slope survives. β2 multiplies the transformed regressor without itself being transformed, so estimating the starred equation returns the coefficient we actually wanted. Only the intercept is rescaled, and β1 = β1*/(1 − ρ) recovers it.

The practical difficulty. ρ is unknown. The transformation requires the very parameter whose existence we were testing for, so it must be estimated — typically by regressing ût on ût−1 — which makes the procedure feasible GLS rather than GLS, with large-sample rather than exact properties. Practitioners iterate: re-estimate ρ̂ from the new residuals, redo the transformation, and continue until ρ̂ settles. Cochrane–Orcutt and Prais–Winsten are the two standard implementations, differing in whether the first observation is dropped or rescaled by √(1 − ρ2) — which matters in a short sample, and time series samples are short.
