OLS Assumptions and Goodness of Fit

What OLS needs in order to be trusted, and how much of Y the model actually explains.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • state all seven OLS assumptions and say what each one is protecting;
  • show that β̂2 is a linear estimator, and derive the three properties of its weights ki;
  • prove that β̂2 is unbiased, and derive var(β̂2), var(β̂1) and cov(β̂1, β̂2);
  • explain why σ̂2 = Σûi2/(n − 2) and not Σûi2/n;
  • show that OLS has minimum variance among linear unbiased estimators;
  • derive r2 from TSS = ESS + RSS, and interpret a fitted equation reported in the house format;
  • say why a high r2 is not evidence of a good model.

If those two formulas and the fact that Σxi = 0 are not automatic yet, go back before starting section 4 — the derivations below use them on nearly every line.

1 · Why the assumptions come first

Unit 1B produced two estimators. It did not establish that they are any good. Before examining the reliability of the population estimates derived from the OLS method, we need to make a few simplifying assumptions.

Recall that the functional form of the population regression function has to be specified before estimation — for example

Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui

The model clearly states that Yi depends linearly on Xi and ui. What it does not state is anything about the process generating the data. That gap matters: statistical inference is impossible without a clear view of what constitutes desirable statistical properties of the data generation process. The assumptions below fill the gap. Each one is a claim about the disturbances and the regressors, not about the estimator, and every result in the rest of this unit is bought with one or more of them.

2 · The seven assumptions

1 The regression model is linear in parameters. The βs are not raised to a power greater or less than one. A model can be non-linear in variables and still satisfy this.
2 The values of Xi are fixed / non-stochastic. X is fixed — probably by design — and therefore not a random variable. Where X is stochastic, it is treated as fixed in repeated sampling.
3 No relationship between the regressors and the disturbance: cov(Xi, ui) = 0.
4 The disturbance has a mean of zero: E(ui | Xi) = E(ui) = 0. Given any value of Xi, the average of the disturbances is zero.
5 Homoscedasticity — constant variance of ui: var(ui) = E[ui − E(ui | Xi)]2 = E(ui2) = σ2. The variance of the disturbances is the same throughout the sample.
6 No autocorrelation between disturbances: given Xi and Xj with their disturbances ui and uj, once i ≠ j the correlation between the two is zero — cov(ui, uj | Xi, Xj) = 0.
7 No perfect collinearity of the regressors. It must not be possible to express any independent variable as a deterministic function of another.

Assumption 1 — linear in parameters, three examples

The assumption restricts how the parameters enter, and says nothing at all about how the variables enter:

a Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui — linear in parameters and in the variable. Satisfies it.
b Yi = β1 + β2Xi2 + ui — linear in parameters, non-linear in the variable. Satisfies it: set Zi = Xi2 and it is an ordinary simple regression.
c Yi = β12 + β2Xi2 + ui — non-linear in parameters as well as in the variable. Violates it.

Assumption 2 — fixed in repeated sampling

Taking X as fixed is a strong claim about the world, and in most economic data it is plainly false: income is not set by the researcher. The working version is weaker and is the one to remember. Fixed in repeated sampling means that if we could draw the sample again and again, we would hold the same set of X values each time and let only the disturbances — and therefore the Ys — change. Every expectation taken below is an expectation of that kind: over disturbances, with X held where it is. That is why Xi and any function of it can be pulled outside an expectation operator as though it were a constant, a step used repeatedly from section 4 onwards.

Assumptions 3 and 4 — a zero mean, at every X

Consider the relationship between consumption (Y) and income (X). The figure below is a population regression function: at each level of income, several different levels of consumption were recorded. Although people may have the same income, their consumption differs for many known and unknown reasons unrelated to income.

Some observations sit above the population regression line and some below — positive disturbances and negative disturbances. Assumption 4 says that at each level of income these cancel, so that the average consumption at that income lies exactly on the line. The ringed points in the figure are those conditional averages.

Assumptions 3 and 4 are not the same claim cov(Xi, ui) = 0 rules out a linear association between the regressor and the disturbance. E(ui | Xi) = 0 rules out any systematic relationship, linear or not, and implies the covariance condition. The second is therefore stronger. When assumption 3 fails, X is called endogenous, OLS is biased, and no amount of extra data repairs it — this is Unit 3B, and it is the most serious failure in the list.

Assumption 5 — constant variance

The spread of the disturbances is the same at every value of X. In the figure above, press the button and the spread fans out with income instead: rich households vary far more in their consumption than poor ones. The rings stay on the line — OLS remains unbiased — but every variance formula derived in section 6 stops being correct, because each of them assumes a single σ2 that applies everywhere. This is heteroscedasticity, Unit 2C.

Assumption 6 — no autocorrelation

Knowing that one disturbance was positive should tell you nothing about the next. In cross-section data that is usually reasonable. In time series it very often is not: a shock to consumption this quarter tends to persist into the next.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It plots a sequence of disturbances against observation number, with a control for the correlation between consecutive disturbances; at zero the signs alternate freely, and at high values they arrive in long runs.

Assumption 7 — no perfect collinearity

With more than one regressor, it should not be possible to write any of them as a deterministic function of the others. In

Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui

there must be no relationship such as X3i = 2X2i. If there were, the two regressors would carry identical information and there would be no way to say how much of the movement in Y belongs to each — the data simply cannot separate them. This has a visible trace in the simple model already: β̂2 = Σxiyi / Σxi2 does not exist when X does not vary, because the denominator is zero. Perfect collinearity is the several-regressor version of the same breakdown, and the imperfect version of it is Unit 2B.

3 · Precision: what a standard error is

It should never escape the student's mind that OLS relies on sampled data to make inferences about population parameters. The estimators derived from it are therefore random variables: as the sample changes, so do the OLS estimates. Before judging the precision of an estimate we have to account for that uncertainty, and the tool statistics provides is the standard error.

Definition The standard error is nothing but the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimator. The sampling distribution of an estimator is simply a probability or frequency distribution of the estimator — the distribution of the set of values it takes across all possible samples of the same size drawn from a given population. Sampling distributions are used to draw inferences about the values of the population parameters on the basis of the values of the estimators calculated from one or more samples.

Two properties have to be established before that standard error can be derived: linearity and unbiasedness. They are the L and the U of BLUE, and they come first because the variance formula in section 6 is built on top of them.

4 · Linearity of the OLS estimator

  1. Start from the estimator derived in Unit 1B and split the fraction:

    β̂2 = Σ xiyi / Σ xi2 = Σ ( xi / Σ xi2 ) yi = Σ ki yi = Σ ki Yi (Equation 1)

    where

    ki = xi / Σ xi2

    Why Σkiyi and ΣkiYi are the same thing
    Σkiyi = Σki(Yi − Ȳ) = ΣkiYi − ȲΣki, and Σki = 0 by the first property below. So the second term vanishes.
  2. Equation 1 says that β̂2 is a linear function — a weighted average of the Yi, with weights ki. Because X is fixed (assumption 2), the ki are constants, and they have three properties that do almost all the work below:

    Σ ki = 0  ·  Σ kiXi = Σ kixi = 1  ·  Σ ki2 = 1 / Σ xi2

    All three, in one line each

    Σki = 0. Σki = Σxi / Σxi2, and the numerator Σxi = Σ(Xi − X̄) = 0 by the definition of the mean.

    Σkixi = 1. Σkixi = Σxi2 / Σxi2 = 1. And ΣkiXi = Σki(xi + X̄) = Σkixi + X̄Σki = 1 + 0 = 1.

    Σki2 = 1/Σxi2. Σki2 = Σxi2 / (Σxi2)2 = 1 / Σxi2. Note that the denominator is squared because the whole constant Σxi2 is squared along with xi.

5 · Unbiasedness

  1. Substitute the PRF, Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui, into Equation 1:

    β̂2 = Σ ki(β1 + β2Xi + ui) = β1Σki + β2ΣkiXi + Σkiui (Equation 2)

  2. Apply the properties of ki: the first term is zero and the second collapses to β2. So

    β̂2 = β2 + Σ kiui

    This one line is worth more than the rest of the section. The estimate equals the truth plus a weighted sum of the disturbances — the sampling error. It is the reason β̂2 is a random variable, and it reappears in every derivation below.
  3. Take expectations, using E(ui) = 0 from assumption 4 and holding the ki fixed by assumption 2:

    E(β̂2) = β2 + Σ kiE(ui) = β2 (Equation 3)

What unbiasedness does and does not promise E(β̂2) = β2 says that on average across all possible samples the estimator hits the true parameter. It says nothing whatever about the sample in front of you, which may be far off. That is precisely why the variance comes next: unbiasedness locates the centre of the sampling distribution, and the variance says how tightly the distribution is packed around it.

6 · Variance and covariance of the estimators

  1. Begin from the definition of variance, and use the result of section 5:

    var(β̂2) = E[β̂2 − E(β̂2)]2 = E[β̂2 − β2]2 = E(Σ kiui)2 (Equation 4)

  2. Expand the square of the sum into squared terms and cross terms:

    E(Σ kiui)2 = Σ ki2E(ui2) + Σi≠j kikjE(uiuj)

    Assumption 6 kills every cross term, since E(uiuj) = 0 for i ≠ j. Assumption 5 replaces each E(ui2) with the same σ2.
  3. What survives is Σki2σ2, and the third property of the weights finishes it:

    var(β̂2) = σ2 / Σ xi2  ·  se(β̂2) = σ / √( Σ xi2 ) (Equation 5)

  4. The same route, applied to β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄, gives the intercept's variance and the covariance between the two estimators (Equation 6):

    var(β̂1) = σ2 · Σ Xi2 / ( n Σ xi2 )  ·  cov(β̂1, β̂2) = − X̄ · σ2 / Σ xi2 = − X̄ · var(β̂2)

    Where the covariance comes from

    Averaging the PRF gives Ȳ = β1 + β2X̄ + ū, so β1 = Ȳ − β2X̄ − ū. Subtracting this from β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄ leaves

    β̂1 − β1 = −X̄(β̂2 − β2) + ū

    Multiply by (β̂2 − β2) and take expectations. The second piece is E[ū Σkiui] = (σ2/n)Σki = 0, so cov(β̂1, β̂2) = −X̄ E[(β̂2 − β2)2] = −X̄ var(β̂2).

    The negative sign is worth a moment: since the fitted line must pass through (X̄, Ȳ), a sample that produces a steeper slope must produce a lower intercept to compensate. The two estimates are not free to move independently.

Three levers on precision — read them off Equation 5 var(β̂2) = σ2/Σxi2 falls when σ2 is small (less unexplained noise in Y), when X is spread widely about its mean (a large Σxi2), and when n is large — since every extra observation adds another non-negative term to Σxi2. Note what this says about experimental design: variation in the regressor is not a nuisance, it is the thing that buys precision. A sample in which every household earns roughly the same income can tell you almost nothing about the slope.
Everything so far depends on σ2, which nobody has The variance and covariance of the OLS estimators all depend on the variance of the disturbance, E(ui2) = σ2 — a population quantity, and unknown. This is a clear demonstration of how the sampling error introduced in the sample regression function shows up in the OLS estimators. To get any further, σ2 has to be estimated from the sample as well.

7 · Estimating σ2

The natural guess is to use the residuals, since ûi is the only observable stand-in for ui. The derivation below shows that the natural guess needs one correction — the divisor is n − 2, not n.

  1. Start from the population regression function (Equation 7):

    Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui

    Averaging over the sample gives (Equation 8):

    Ȳ = β1 + β2X̄ + ū

  2. Subtracting Equation 8 from Equation 7 gives the PRF in deviation form (Equation 9):

    yi = β2xi + (ui − ū)

  3. The SRF can be written in deviation form too (Equation 10):

    yi = β̂2xi + ûi

  4. Setting the two expressions for yi equal and rearranging (Equation 11):

    ûi = − (β̂2 − β2)xi + (ui − ū)

  5. Square and sum over the sample (Equation 12):

    Σ ûi2 = (β̂2 − β2)2 Σ xi2 − 2(β̂2 − β2) Σ xi(ui − ū) + Σ (ui − ū)2

  6. Take expectations of both sides (Equation 13). Each term is already known:

    E[(β̂2 − β2)2] Σxi2 = ( σ2 / Σxi2 ) Σxi2 = σ2

    E[ 2(β̂2 − β2) Σ xi(ui − ū) ] = 2σ2

    E[ Σ (ui − ū)2 ] = (n − 1)σ2

    The middle term, in full

    Σxi(ui − ū) = Σxiui − ūΣxi = Σxiui, because Σxi = 0. And from section 5, β̂2 − β2 = Σkiui = Σxiui / Σxi2. So the term is

    2 E[ (Σxiui)2 ] / Σxi2 = 2 σ2 Σxi2 / Σxi2 = 2σ2

    using assumptions 5 and 6 once more to clear the cross terms out of the squared sum. The last of the three results, E[Σ(ui − ū)2] = (n − 1)σ2, is the standard sample-variance result from ECON 2026.

  7. Collecting them (Equation 14):

    E( Σ ûi2 ) = σ2 − 2σ2 + (n − 1)σ2 = (n − 2)σ2

  8. So dividing the residual sum of squares by n − 2 defines an estimator (Equation 15) that is unbiased for σ2 (Equation 16):

    σ̂2 = Σ ûi2 / (n − 2)  with  E(σ̂2) = σ2

Degrees of freedom The residuals are not free to be anything: the two normal equations of Unit 1B impose Σûi = 0 and ΣûiXi = 0. Fix any n − 2 of them and the last two are determined. Two degrees of freedom have been spent estimating β̂1 and β̂2, which is why n − 2 is the divisor here and why the same n − 2 turns up as the degrees of freedom of the t-test in Unit 1F. In multiple regression with k parameters the divisor becomes n − k.
Replacing σ2 with σ̂2 in Equation 5 gives the estimated standard error, se(β̂2) = σ̂ / √(Σxi2) — the number reported in the parentheses beneath every coefficient in this course.

8 · Minimum variance — the last leg of Gauss–Markov

Linearity and unbiasedness are established. What remains is to show that OLS has the smallest variance in the class of linear unbiased estimators. The strategy is to invent a rival and prove it cannot win.

  1. The OLS estimator, with its weights (Equation 17):

    β̂2 = Σ kiYi,   ki = xi / Σ xi2

    Now suppose someone proposes an alternative (Equation 18), also a weighted average of Y but with weights wi not necessarily equal to ki:

    β2* = Σ wiYi

  2. Substitute the PRF and take expectations (Equation 19):

    E(β2*) = β1Σwi + β2ΣwiXi

    For β2* to be unbiased, the rival's weights must satisfy exactly the same two conditions the ki do:

    Σ wi = 0  ·  Σ wiXi = 1

  3. Its variance follows the same route as Equation 5:

    var(β2*) = σ2 Σ wi2

    Now write wi as the OLS weight plus a deviation from it, and expand:

    Σ wi2 = Σ (wi − ki + ki)2 = Σ (wi − ki)2 + 2Σ(wi − ki)ki + Σ ki2

  4. The cross term is zero.
    Why the cross term vanishes

    Σ(wi − ki)ki = Σwiki − Σki2. For the first piece,

    Σ wiki = Σ wixi / Σ xi2 = ( ΣwiXi − X̄Σwi ) / Σ xi2 = 1 / Σ xi2

    using the two unbiasedness conditions on the wi. But Σki2 is also 1/Σxi2, so the two pieces cancel exactly. This step is where unbiasedness of the rival is spent — it is not a free algebraic simplification.

    What is left is

    var(β2*) = σ2 Σ (wi − ki)2 + σ2 Σ ki2 = σ2 Σ (wi − ki)2 + var(β̂2)

  5. The second term is fixed — it is the OLS variance, and nothing the rival does can change it. So the only way to minimise var(β2*) is to minimise the first term, a sum of squares, whose smallest possible value is zero and which reaches zero only when

    wi = ki for every i

The takeaway When the weights wi equal the least-squares weights ki, the two estimators have the same variance — they are the same estimator. In every other case var(β2*) > var(β̂2). Put simply, β̂2 is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator of β2 in the class of linear estimators. Linear, unbiased, minimum variance: the Gauss–Markov theorem of Unit 1B is now proved rather than asserted.

9 · Goodness of fit: the coefficient of determination

Up to this point the concern has been deriving estimators for the population regression function, and establishing that they are BLUE. A different question remains. The sample regression function approximates the population regression function; we need some way of determining how well the sample regression line fits the sampled data. That is goodness of fit.

Picture the variation in Y and the variation in X as two circles. The degree of overlap is the part of the movement in Y that the movement in X accounts for. r2 measures that overlap.

  1. Every observation splits into a fitted value and a residual (Equation 20), and the same holds in deviation form (Equation 21):

    Yi = Ŷi + ûi  ⟹  yi = ŷi + ûi

  2. Square and sum over the whole sample:

    Σ yi2 = Σ ŷi2 + Σ ûi2 + 2 Σ ŷiûi

    The cross term disappears, because Σŷiûi = 0 — recall ŷi = β̂2xi, so Σŷiûi = β̂2Σxiûi, and the normal equations force Σxiûi = 0. The fitted values and the residuals carry no information in common; that is what it means for OLS to have squeezed everything out of X.
  3. The three surviving terms each have a name (Equation 22):
    TSS Σyi2 = Σ(Yi − Ȳ)2 — the total variation of Y about its mean.
    ESS Σŷi2 = Σ(Ŷi − Ȳ)2 = β̂22Σxi2 — the variation of the fitted values about the mean, i.e. the part explained by the regression.
    RSS Σûi2 — the unexplained variation left in the residuals.

    TSS = ESS + RSS

  4. Divide throughout by TSS (Equation 23):

    1 = ESS/TSS + RSS/TSS

    and define the coefficient of determination as the explained share:

    r2 = ESS/TSS = 1 − RSS/TSS = β̂22 Σ xi2 / Σ yi2

  5. An equivalent expression, useful when you already have the raw sums (Equation 24):

    r2 = ( Σ xiyi )2 / ( Σ xi2 · Σ yi2 )

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It shows a scatter with the sample mean of Y, the fitted line and the residual drops, together with a bar splitting the total sum of squares into its explained and residual parts as the scatter is widened or narrowed.

Three things to know about r2 i. r2 is never negative — it is a ratio of two sums of squares.
ii. It is bounded: 0 ≤ r2 ≤ 1.
iii. A value of 1 is a perfect fit, with every observation on the line; a value of 0 means no linear relationship between the dependent and independent variable — that is, β̂2 = 0.
The correlation coefficient is recovered as r = √(r2), taking the sign of β̂2: a fit of r2 = 0.80 on a downward-sloping line gives r = −0.89, not +0.89. In the simple two-variable model only — with more than one regressor there is no single r to recover.

10 · Interpreting the fitted regression

In the population regression function Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui, β1 is the intercept — the value of Yi when Xi = 0 — and β2 is the slope, telling you how much Yi changes for a one unit change in Xi. Formally β2 is the derivative of Y with respect to X:

∂Yi / ∂Xi = β2,   assuming ∂ui / ∂Xi = 0

That side condition is not decoration. It is assumption 3 doing its work: the slope can be read as "the change in Y for a one unit change in X" only if nothing in the disturbance moves when X moves.

A worked example

An estimated simple regression of consumption on income:

Reading every number in that display 90.18 — when income is zero, predicted consumption is 90.18.
0.75 — for every additional dollar of income, consumption rises by 75 cents. This is the marginal propensity to consume.
(0.12) and (0.20) — the standard errors of β̂1 and β̂2, showing that there is uncertainty attached to both estimates. The average deviation of the estimator from its mean is 12 cents and 20 cents respectively.
751.50 and 3.75 — the t-statistics, each the coefficient divided by its own standard error. What they are used for is the subject of Unit 1F; the house reporting format carries them from the start so the habit is formed early.
r2 = 0.80 — the regression explains 80% of the variation in consumption. Equivalently, the variation in income accounts for 80% of the variation in consumption.
r = 0.89 — the correlation coefficient, √0.80, positive because the slope is.
Reproduce this layout in the exam Coefficients on the first line, standard errors in parentheses directly beneath their own coefficient, t-statistics below those, and r2 reported alongside. A list of numbers with no indication of which belongs to which parameter earns very little, however correct the arithmetic behind it.

11 · Why a high r2 is not a good model

The r2 of 0.80 above suggests the consumption model fits well. Students should not be quick to judge a model good on that basis. It is quite possible for a model that is useless from a theoretical perspective to have a very high r2.

The standard demonstration: the divorce rate in Maine and per-capita consumption of margarine move together almost perfectly over the 2000s, with a correlation coefficient of r = 0.9926. Regress one on the other and r2 = 0.9852 — a fit far better than almost anything in applied economics. The regression is nonetheless worthless. Nothing links the two series; they simply both trended downwards over the same decade.

What r2 is, and what it is not r2 measures how closely the observations cluster around the fitted line. It is not a test of whether the model is correctly specified, whether the right variables are included, whether the assumptions of section 2 hold, or whether X causes Y. A model with a low r2 may have a perfectly reliable and interesting slope coefficient; a model with a high r2 may be nonsense. Judge a specification on the theory behind it, the diagnostic tests of Unit 2, and the significance of individual coefficients — then look at r2.

12 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

Prove the three properties of the weights: Σki = 0, ΣkiXi = 1 and Σki2 = 1/Σxi2. For each one, state where in the unit it is used.
Show solution

Σki = 0. ki = xi / Σxi2, so Σki = Σxi / Σxi2, and Σxi = Σ(Xi − X̄) = 0. Used in section 5 to kill the β1Σki term, and again in the covariance derivation.

ΣkiXi = 1. Write Xi = xi + X̄. Then ΣkiXi = Σkixi + X̄Σki = Σxi2/Σxi2 + 0 = 1. Used in section 5 to reduce β2ΣkiXi to β2, which is the step that delivers unbiasedness.

Σki2 = 1/Σxi2. Σki2 = Σxi2 / (Σxi2)2 = 1/Σxi2. Used in section 6 to turn σ2Σki2 into σ2/Σxi2, and again in section 8 to cancel the cross term.

Show that β̂2 = β2 + Σkiui, and use it to explain in words why β̂2 is a random variable, why it is unbiased, and which assumption you relied on for each of the two claims.
Show solution

Substituting the PRF into β̂2 = ΣkiYi gives β1Σki + β2ΣkiXi + Σkiui. The first term is zero and the second is β2, leaving β̂2 = β2 + Σkiui.

Random. β2 is a fixed population constant; the estimator differs from it by Σkiui, a weighted sum of the disturbances. Because the disturbances are random and differ from sample to sample, so does β̂2. This relies on assumption 2 — the ki are constants, so all the randomness sits in u.

Unbiased. E(β̂2) = β2 + ΣkiE(ui) = β2, which needs assumption 4, E(ui) = 0. Note that neither homoscedasticity nor the absence of autocorrelation is needed for unbiasedness — those are spent on the variance in section 6, which is exactly why heteroscedastic errors leave OLS unbiased but inefficient.

A researcher wants a more precise estimate of the slope and can afford to collect more data. Using var(β̂2) = σ2/Σxi2, advise them on how to choose the new observations, and say what they cannot fix by sampling.
Show solution

Precision improves as Σxi2 = Σ(Xi − X̄)2 rises. More observations help, but where they are taken matters as much as how many: observations at values of X far from X̄ add large terms to the sum, while observations bunched near X̄ add almost nothing. The advice is to deliberately sample across a wide range of X — for a consumption function, to include both low- and high-income households rather than concentrating on the middle.

What sampling cannot fix is σ2, the variance of the disturbance. That is a feature of the population relationship — how much of Y is driven by things outside the model — not of the sample. Reducing it means improving the model: better measurement, or explanatory variables that are currently sitting in u. That is the subject of Unit 2A.

Explain why σ̂2 divides by n − 2. What would go wrong if Σûi2/n were used instead, and in which direction would the reported standard errors be wrong?
Show solution

Section 7 shows E(Σûi2) = (n − 2)σ2. Dividing by n − 2 therefore produces an estimator whose expected value is exactly σ2. The intuition is degrees of freedom: the residuals must satisfy Σûi = 0 and ΣûiXi = 0, two restrictions imposed by the normal equations, so only n − 2 of them are free to vary. Two were spent estimating β̂1 and β̂2.

Using n instead gives E(Σûi2/n) = (n − 2)σ2/n, which is smaller than σ2 — the estimator is biased downwards. Standard errors would therefore be reported too small, t-statistics too large, and coefficients would look more significant than the evidence warrants. The bias shrinks as n grows, which is why the correction matters most in exactly the small samples where it is most tempting to ignore it.

Two regressions of the same dependent variable are reported. Model A has r2 = 0.94 and a slope coefficient with a t-statistic of 0.6. Model B has r2 = 0.31 and a slope with a t-statistic of 5.2. Which would you rather have, and what else would you want to know?
Show solution

Model B, on this evidence. r2 reports how tightly the observations cluster around the line; it says nothing about whether the estimated relationship is reliable. Model A's slope is indistinguishable from zero — its high r2 is being produced by something other than a dependable link between X and Y, very often a shared trend, which is precisely the margarine-and-divorce case in section 11. Model B has a low fit but a slope estimated precisely enough to build on; a low r2 is common and unalarming in cross-section data, where individual behaviour is genuinely noisy.

What else to ask. What each model says theoretically — a coefficient worth having must correspond to a mechanism you can name. Whether the assumptions of section 2 survive testing, since a significant t-statistic computed from a wrong variance formula is not evidence of anything (Units 2C and 2D). Whether either regression is in levels of trending series. What the units are, so that "0.75" can be judged economically and not just statistically. And the sample size, without which neither t-statistic can be placed against a critical value.

13 · Check yourself

The variance of the disturbance rises steadily with income. Which assumption has failed?
Correct — this is heteroscedasticity. OLS stays unbiased, but every variance formula in section 6 is now wrong.
Assumption 5 is the constant-variance assumption, var(ui) = σ². A spread that fans out with X leaves the conditional means on the line — so unbiasedness survives — but invalidates var(β̂₂) = σ²/Σx².
Given var(β̂2) = σ2/Σxi2, and holding everything else fixed, the slope is estimated more precisely when
Correct — Σxi² sits in the denominator, so a widely spread regressor shrinks the variance of the estimator.
Σxi² = Σ(Xi − X̄)² is the denominator, so anything that makes X vary more makes β̂₂ more precise. Observations bunched near X̄ contribute almost nothing, which is why sampling across a wide range of X is worth designing for.
In σ̂2 = Σûi2/(n − 2), the divisor is n − 2 because
Correct — E(Σû²) = (n − 2)σ², because the normal equations impose two restrictions on the residuals.
Nothing is dropped from the sum. All n residuals are used, but they must satisfy Σûi = 0 and ΣûiXi = 0, so only n − 2 of them are free — which is exactly what E(Σû²) = (n − 2)σ² records.
Margarine consumption and the divorce rate in Maine give r2 = 0.985. This tells you
Correct — r² measures clustering about the line and nothing else. Both series simply trended in the same direction.
r² measures how closely the observations sit around the fitted line. It is not a test of specification, of the assumptions, or of causation — and there is no "goodness-of-fit criterion" a regression can pass. Two series that trend together will produce a high r² whether or not anything connects them.
