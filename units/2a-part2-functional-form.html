Misspecification: Functional Form

Logs, quadratics and the RESET test — getting the shape of the relationship right.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • say why the constant term should never be suppressed, and why it should not be relied on either;
  • distinguish non-linearity in the variables from non-linearity in the parameters;
  • find the marginal effect and the turning point of a quadratic specification;
  • linearise a Cobb–Douglas production function by taking logs;
  • interpret β2 in the linear, double-log, log–lin and lin–log forms;
  • build and interpret an interaction term;
  • use RESET to test functional form, and the Chow test to test parameter stability;
  • run the test of variances that the Chow test's own assumptions require.
The second of the three specification choices Part 1 dealt with choosing the independent variables. After that, the next step is choosing the functional form of the relationship between each independent variable and the dependent variable. A multiple regression model suffers from functional form misspecification when it does not properly account for the relationship between the dependent and the observed explanatory variables.
Theoretical considerations usually dictate the form. The basic technique is to choose the shape that best exemplifies the expected underlying economic principles, and then use the mathematical form that produces that shape.

1 · The constant term

Sometimes β1 is of genuine theoretical importance. Take the cost of producing output:

Ci = β1 + β2Qi + εi

Here β1 represents total fixed cost — the cost when Qi = 0. What about a product with few, if any, fixed costs? Do you remove the constant?

Do not suppress the constant term

i Including the constant is what ensures E(u) = 0. Consider Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui where E(u) = α ≠ 0. Rewriting ui = α + vi with E(v) = 0 gives an equation with intercept (β1 + α) and a well-behaved error. The constant simply absorbs α — but only if it is there to do so.
ii In most cases suppressing the constant results in misspecification, since the true intercept is hardly ever exactly zero.
iii If you omit the constant term and it is not zero, its impact is forced into the other coefficients — the slope has to do the intercept's job as well as its own.

But do not rely on estimates of it either

Two reasons the intercept should not be leaned on for analysis:

i It captures the average value of Y when all the independent variables are zero. It is unlikely that every variable in a model can take the value zero — nobody has zero years of age — so estimates of the intercept are tenuous extrapolations well outside the data.
ii The constant term is a garbage collector. Including it allows the constant to absorb the non-zero mean of the error term arising from specification error — which is exactly what you want it to do, and exactly why its own value cannot then be trusted.
The rule, stated together Always include the constant. Never interpret it. The two pieces of advice are not in tension: the intercept earns its place by protecting the slopes, and the same quality that makes it protective — it absorbs whatever is left over — is what makes its own value uninformative.

2 · When the true model is non-linear in the variables

The OLS assumption is linearity in the parameters, not in the independent variables. Often the model is non-linear in the explanatory variables, and that is entirely permitted.

Return to the cost function. Microeconomic theory tells us that for most goods the relationship between total cost and output is non-linear — specifically cubic:

Ci = β1 + β2Qi + β3Qi2 + β4Qi3 + ui

with theory predicting β2 > 0, β3 < 0 and β4 > 0 — the familiar S-shaped total cost curve.

Leaving out the non-linear terms is underfitting If the true model is non-linear and we omit the non-linear functions, we have omitted relevant right-hand-side variables — the model is underfitted, and everything in Part 1 applies. Q2 and Q3 are certainly correlated with Q, so the bias is guaranteed rather than merely possible.

The consequence is that the estimated impact of the independent variable is simply wrong. Under the linear model the marginal cost is a constant:

dC/dQ = β2

whereas under the correct specification it varies with the level of output:

dC/dQ = β2 + 2β3Qi + 3β4Qi2

A linear model does not merely mis-estimate marginal cost; it asserts that marginal cost is the same at every level of output, which is a substantive economic claim nobody made.

Quadratic forms

Cubic functions occur, but quadratic forms are more popular — at least in economic theory:

Y = β1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + β4X32 + β5X4 + … + u

Quadratics take U or inverted-U shapes, depending on whether the coefficient on the squared term is positive or negative. U-shaped means an impact that falls and then rises; inverted U means it rises and then falls.

Finding the turning point from the regression output With Y = β1 + β2X + β3X2 + u, differentiate and set to zero:

dY/dX = β2 + 2β3X = 0  ⟹  X* = − β̂2 / 2β̂3

β̂3 < 0 makes X* a maximum; β̂3 > 0 makes it a minimum. Always check that X* lies inside the range of your data — a turning point far outside it is arithmetic, not economics.

3 · When the model is non-linear in the parameters

So far the non-linearity has been in the variables, which OLS tolerates. What happens when the model is non-linear in the parameters, which it does not?

Take one of the most famous functions in economics, the Cobb–Douglas production function:

Q = F(L, K, u) = β1 Lβ2 Kβ3 eu

This violates the linearity-in-parameters assumption outright: β2 and β3 appear as exponents. What would happen if we simply estimated Q = β1 + β2L + β3K + u instead? We would be estimating a different model — one that assumes constant marginal products and additive rather than multiplicative technology, which is not the theory we started from.

The fix is to take natural logarithms:

ln Q = ln β1 + β2 ln L + β3 ln K + u

which is the double-log form — logs of variables on both sides. The parameters now enter linearly, and OLS applies. Note that the multiplicative error eu becomes an additive u, which is exactly what the classical assumptions require; this is why Cobb–Douglas is conventionally written with a multiplicative disturbance.

4 · The four functional forms

Double-log

ln Y = β1 + β2 ln X2 + β3 ln X3 + u

It does not only linearise a non-linear function. The individual coefficients can be interpreted directly as elasticities:

βk = Δ(ln Y) / Δ(ln Xk) = (ΔY/Y) / (ΔXk/Xk) = elasticity of Y with respect to Xk

So a 1% change in X2 is associated with a β̂2% change in Y.

Why economists log so much Logging is not confined to cases where we suspect non-linearity in the parameters. Frequently economists are interested in elasticities rather than partial or marginal effects, and logging both sides is the direct way to estimate one.
But you cannot log everything. Some variables contain negative or zero values, and ln is undefined there. And sometimes it simply does not make intuitive sense to log a variable — an unemployment rate already in percent, or a variable measured in years.

Semi-log form 1 — log–lin

ln Y = β1 + β2X + u

The dependent variable is logged; one or more of the regressors are not. Generally β2 · 100 ≈ %ΔY / ΔX, which is called a semi-elasticity: the percentage change in Y when X changes by one unit is approximately 100β2.

The approximation, and the exact figure 100β2 works best for small values of β2. The exact percentage change is

%ΔY / ΔX = 100 [ eβ2 − 1 ]

For β2 = 0.05 the approximation gives 5% and the exact value 5.13% — negligible. For β2 = 0.40 the approximation gives 40% and the exact value 49.2%, which is not. Quote the exact form whenever the coefficient is large.

This is the popular form for models of wage determination — the Mincer equation:

ln wage = β1 + β2 Education + β3 Experience + β4 Experience2 + u

Semi-log form 2 — lin–log

Y = β1 + β2 ln X + u,   for X > 0

Generally ΔY = (β2/100) · %ΔX. So when X increases by 1%, Y changes by approximately β2/100 units. An example is a labour supply function: hours worked = β1 + β2 ln wage + u.

The four forms, and what β2 means in each
LinearY = β1 + β2X ΔX of 1 unit ⟹ ΔY of β2 units
Double-logln Y = β1 + β2 ln X ΔX of 1% ⟹ ΔY of β2%  (elasticity)
Log–linln Y = β1 + β2X ΔX of 1 unit ⟹ ΔY of 100β2%  (semi-elasticity)
Lin–logY = β1 + β2 ln X ΔX of 1% ⟹ ΔY of β2/100 units
The pattern is worth reading off rather than memorising: a logged variable turns its own side of the sentence into a percentage.

5 · Interaction terms

Sometimes the impact of a variable depends on the level of some other variable. Consider the Mincer equation again, relating wages to years of schooling and years of experience:

ln wage = β1 + β2 educ + β3 exper + β4 exper2 + u

This model implies the marginal return to experience depends only on how much experience the person has, holding education constant. But the return to experience might also depend on how much schooling the worker has: someone with a university education may be able to move up faster than someone with only secondary schooling.

To capture that, include an interaction term — simply the product of the two variables:

ln wage = β1 + β2 educ + β3 exper + β4 exper2 + β5 (exper · educ) + u

The partial derivative with respect to experience becomes

∂ ln wage / ∂ exper = β3 + 2β4 exper + β5 educ

What the interaction has bought The effect of experience now depends on both the level of experience and the level of schooling. If β5 is positive, the effect of experience is greater for people with more schooling.
Note what this does to interpretation: once an interaction is in the model, β3 alone is no longer "the effect of experience" — it is the effect of experience for someone with zero years of education, which may be nobody in the sample. Report the derivative evaluated at a meaningful value, not the raw coefficient.

6 · Testing the functional form

The Ramsey RESET test from Part 1 does double duty. It can also be used to detect general functional form misspecification — should some of our variables be squared or cubed? Should we include interaction terms?

The mechanism explains why. Ŷ is a linear combination of the regressors, so Ŷ2 and Ŷ3 contain their squares and cross-products — precisely the quadratic and interaction terms that a functional-form error consists of leaving out. Adding them and testing H0: γ1 = γ2 = 0 asks whether those shapes would have helped.

Which is why a RESET rejection is ambiguous RESET is sensitive to omitted variables (Part 1) and to incorrect functional form (here), for the same underlying reason. A rejection tells you the model is inadequate but not which of the two diagnoses applies — and the remedies are completely different. Use theory to decide which is more plausible before reaching for a fix.

7 · Parameter stability and structural change

The examples so far have been cross-sectional. More often than not we have data with a time dimension, and then a further possibility arises: the relationship itself may have changed part-way through the sample. That is structural change.

i The "free education for all" policy of the 1960s — did it change the dynamic between education and earnings in Barbados?
ii Has the relationship between consumption and income changed since 2008, under recessionary conditions?

Suppose we have quarterly data on household consumption and income from 1992 to 2016 — 25 years, so 100 observations. We can assume no difference in the relationship and estimate one regression on the full sample:

Ct = β1 + β2Yt + ut

A less optimistic person may argue the recession changed the dynamic, and that two separate regressions are needed:

Ct = δ1 + δ2Yt + u1t   — pre-2008 (1992–2007)

Ct = α1 + α2Yt + u2t   — post-2008 (2008–2016)

The first regression assumes β1 = δ1 = α1 and β2 = δ2 = α2 — no structural change, no difference in parameters. The second and third allow the relationships to differ, so both slopes and intercepts are free to move. Notice that this is exactly the restricted-versus-unrestricted setup of Unit 1F.

8 · The Chow test

Hypotheses and assumptions H0: no structural change — the parameters are stable.
Assumptions: the error terms in the sub-period regressions are normally distributed with the same (homoscedastic) variance, and the two error terms u1t and u2t are independently distributed.
  1. Estimate the model assuming no structural break — the pooled regression. Obtain RSSR with n1 − k degrees of freedom. It is the restricted model because it imposes δ1 = α1 and δ2 = α2.
  2. Estimate the first sub-period regression and collect RSS2, with n2 − k degrees of freedom.
  3. Estimate the second sub-period regression and collect RSS3, with n3 − k degrees of freedom.
  4. Add them: RSSUR = RSS2 + RSS3, with n2 + n3 − 2k degrees of freedom. Then

    F = [ ( RSSR − RSSUR ) / k ] / [ RSSUR / ( n2 + n3 − 2k ) ] ~ Fn2+n3−2kk

The idea If there is in fact no structural change, there is no statistical difference between RSSR and RSSUR — splitting the sample bought nothing. So we do not reject the null of parameter stability if the computed F does not exceed the critical F at the chosen significance level. It is the F-test of Unit 1F with k restrictions, where the restrictions say that each parameter is the same in both periods.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It shows consumption against income across two periods with a control for the size of the structural break, drawing the pooled and the two sub-period regressions and reporting the Chow F statistic and the decision.

Three limitations, all worth stating in an answer i. For the test to be valid the assumptions must be fulfilled — in particular the equal-variance assumption, which needs its own test (section 9).
ii. This version tells you that the regressions differ, not which parameters do. We do not know whether the difference is in the intercepts, the slopes, or both.
iii. The test assumes we know when the structural break occurred. Choosing the break date by looking for the split that maximises F is not this test, and its critical values do not apply.

9 · The test of variances

Examine one of the assumptions underlying the Chow test — that the error variances in the two periods are the same. Since we cannot observe the true error variances, obtain their estimates from the sub-period residual sums of squares:

σ̂12 = RSS1 / (n1 − k)  ·  σ̂22 = RSS2 / (n2 − k)

Given the assumptions underlying the Chow test, these are unbiased estimators of the true variances in the two sub-periods. If σ12 = σ22, as the Chow test assumes, then it can be shown that

( σ̂12 / σ12 ) / ( σ̂22 / σ22 ) ~ F(n1−k), (n2−k)

follows the F distribution with (n1 − k) and (n2 − k) degrees of freedom in numerator and denominator, where k is the number of unknown parameters. Under the null the two population variances cancel, so the test reduces to computing

F = σ̂12 / σ̂22

One practical rule Always put the larger variance in the numerator. This makes F ≥ 1 and lets you use the upper tail of the F-tables, which is the only tail they print. Remember to match the degrees of freedom to whichever sub-period ended up on top.

10 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

A wage equation is estimated as ln wage = 1.42 + 0.084 educ + u. Interpret the coefficient exactly and approximately. Then re-interpret it supposing the equation had instead been wage = 1.42 + 0.084 ln educ + u.
Show solution

Log–lin. The dependent variable is logged and the regressor is not, so β2 is a semi-elasticity. Approximately, one more year of education raises the wage by 100 × 0.084 = 8.4%. Exactly, 100[e0.084 − 1] = 100(1.0876 − 1) = 8.76%. The gap is small here because the coefficient is small; at 0.40 it would be 40% against 49.2%.

Lin–log. Now the regressor is logged and the dependent variable is not, so a 1% rise in education changes the wage by β2/100 = 0.00084 units — whatever wage is measured in. Note this is a far less natural specification for this relationship: it says the effect of an extra year of schooling shrinks as schooling rises, and it makes no use of the fact that wages are naturally thought of in proportional terms.

The general rule: a logged variable turns its own side of the sentence into a percentage.

A Mincer equation gives ln wage = β̂1 + 0.09 educ + 0.061 exper − 0.0009 exper2. At what level of experience do wages peak? Comment on whether the answer is economically sensible, and say what the coefficient on exper alone would mean if an interaction term exper·educ were added.
Show solution

The turning point. Differentiating with respect to experience: ∂ln wage/∂exper = 0.061 + 2(−0.0009)exper = 0.061 − 0.0018 exper. Setting to zero:

exper* = −β̂3/2β̂4 = 0.061 / 0.0018 = 33.9 years

Since β̂4 < 0 this is a maximum: the inverted-U shape. Wages rise with experience but at a diminishing rate, peaking at about 34 years and declining thereafter.

Is it sensible? Yes — 34 years of experience is comfortably inside the working life and inside most samples, and a peak followed by mild decline near retirement is what the literature finds. Had the answer come out at 90 years it would have been arithmetic without economic content, and the quadratic term would be doing nothing but bending the curve within the observed range.

With an interaction. Adding β5(exper·educ) makes the derivative β3 + 2β4exper + β5educ. The coefficient β3 alone is then the effect of experience for a worker with zero years of education — likely nobody in the sample. Report the derivative evaluated at a meaningful education level, such as the sample mean.

A researcher estimating a cost function argues that since the product has no fixed costs, the constant should be dropped. Explain what suppressing it would do, and why the advice "always include it, never interpret it" is not contradictory.
Show solution

What suppressing it does. It forces the regression line through the origin. If the true intercept is not exactly zero — and it hardly ever is — the intercept's job is forced onto the slope coefficients, which tilt to compensate. The estimate of marginal cost is then biased. It also breaks the guarantee that the residuals sum to zero, so the assumption E(u) = 0 is no longer protected by the arithmetic of the fit.

Why the advice is consistent. The two halves are about different things. Include it is about protecting the slopes: the constant absorbs any non-zero mean in the disturbance, so it must be present for the other coefficients to be estimated cleanly. Do not interpret it is about the constant itself: precisely because it is a garbage collector, absorbing whatever specification error leaves behind, its own value carries no reliable meaning. It is also an extrapolation to a point where all regressors are zero, which is usually far outside the data.

On this researcher's specific claim: even if fixed costs really are zero, the correct procedure is to include the constant and test H0: β1 = 0, not to impose it. Imposing a restriction you have not tested is exactly what the general-to-specific approach in Part 1 warns against.

Quarterly data on consumption and income, 1992–2016 (n = 100), with a suspected break at 2008 giving n2 = 64 and n3 = 36. The pooled regression gives RSSR = 1,842; the sub-period regressions give RSS2 = 1,048 and RSS3 = 601. Carry out the Chow test at 5%, and state what you would check before trusting it.
Show solution

Set up. k = 2 (an intercept and a slope). RSSUR = 1,048 + 601 = 1,649, with n2 + n3 − 2k = 100 − 4 = 96 degrees of freedom.

F = [ (1,842 − 1,649) / 2 ] / [ 1,649 / 96 ] = 96.5 / 17.18 = 5.62

Decision. The critical value F296 at 5% is about 3.09. Since 5.62 > 3.09, reject the null of parameter stability: there is evidence of a structural break in the consumption–income relationship at 2008.

What to check first. The Chow test assumes the two sub-period error variances are equal, so run the test of variances: σ̂22 = 1,048/62 = 16.9 and σ̂32 = 601/34 = 17.7. Larger over smaller gives F = 17.7/16.9 = 1.05 on (34, 62) degrees of freedom — nowhere near any critical value, so the equal-variance assumption survives and the Chow result stands.

Two caveats remain. The test says the regressions differ but not how — intercept, slope or both. And 2008 was chosen from prior knowledge of the recession, not from the data; had the break date been picked by searching for the largest F, these critical values would not apply.

Explain why a Cobb–Douglas production function cannot be estimated by OLS as written, why taking logs fixes it, and what the log transformation implies about the error term. Would the same trick work for Q = β1 + β2Lβ3 + u?
Show solution

Why not as written. Q = β1Lβ2Kβ3eu has parameters appearing as exponents, violating assumption 1 — linearity in the parameters. OLS minimises a sum of squares whose first-order conditions are linear in the parameters; here they would not be.

Why logs fix it. ln Q = ln β1 + β2ln L + β3ln K + u. The parameters β2 and β3 now enter linearly, and ln β1 is just a reparameterised intercept — estimate it and exponentiate to recover β1. The model is intrinsically linear: it looked non-linear but a transformation removes it.

What it implies about the error. The multiplicative eu becomes an additive u, which is what the classical assumptions require. This is why the function is conventionally written with a multiplicative disturbance rather than an additive one — with Q = β1Lβ2Kβ3 + u the log transformation would not separate, and no such trick exists.

The second model. No. Q = β1 + β2Lβ3 + u has an additive structure, so logging both sides does not distribute over the sum: ln(β1 + β2Lβ3 + u) cannot be separated into terms linear in the parameters. This is intrinsically non-linear and requires non-linear least squares, which is beyond this course.
