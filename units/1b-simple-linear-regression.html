Simple Linear Regression

The two-variable model, and how OLS picks the line that fits best.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • distinguish the population regression function from the sample regression function, and an estimator from an estimate;
  • state what OLS minimises, and derive β̂1 and β̂2 by both routes;
  • state the four core assumptions of the simple linear regression model;
  • say what BLUE means, term by term, and what the Gauss–Markov theorem claims;
  • explain why a significant coefficient is not evidence of causation.
Assumed from ECON 2026 Much of this unit is a re-introduction. OLS, unbiasedness and goodness of fit were all introduced in Statistical Methods II; expectations were covered in ECON 2025 and 2026. If E(Y | X) does not look familiar, revisit conditional expectation before going further — the whole of this unit rests on it.

1 · What regression is

Regression is a statistical technique that attempts to "explain" movements in one variable — the dependent variable — as a function of movements in one or a set of other variables, ceteris paribus, through the quantification of one or more equations.

Both variables travel under several names, and your textbooks use them interchangeably, so all of them need to be recognisable:

YDependent · explained · response · predicted variable
XIndependent · explanatory · control · predictor variable

A more technical statement of the same thing: regression is concerned with predicting the population mean value of the dependent variable on the basis of known or fixed values of the explanatory variables. That is the conditional population mean:

E(Y | X)

Hold on to the word mean. Regression does not predict what any individual will do. It predicts the average of everyone at a given value of X.

2 · From a line to a regression

A linear equation Y = β1 + β2X describes an exact, deterministic relationship: give it X and it returns Y with no remainder. That is not realistic for economic relationships. Even if much of the variation in Y is caused by X, there is almost always variation that comes from other sources.

A stochastic error term, or disturbance, is added to the linear equation to account for variation in Y that cannot be explained by the included X. It is usually written by adding u, or an epsilon ε, to the equation:

Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui

Reading the equation Yi dependent variable · β1 intercept · β2 slope parameter · Xi independent variable · ui error term
Notation — fix this now In this course β1 is the intercept and β2 the slope. Several diagrams in the textbooks — and one or two reproduced in the lecture slides — come from sources that number from zero, writing β0 for the intercept and β1 for the slope. The mathematics is identical and only the labels differ, but read every figure's axis labels before you trust its subscripts, and use this course's numbering in everything you submit.

The four reasons the error term is there

There are at least four sources of variation in Y not captured by the included X:

iInfluences omitted from the equation
iiMeasurement error
iiiThe true theoretical equation has a different functional form than the one chosen for the regression
ivHuman behaviour can be unpredictable and purely random

The two parts of a regression

Every regression equation splits cleanly in two:

1Deterministic part: β1 + β2Xi — the value of Y determined by a given value of X.
2Stochastic (random) part: ui — everything else.

The deterministic part can be thought of as the expected value of Y given X, which is the conditional mean from section 1:

E(Yi | Xi) = β1 + β2Xi

3 · Regression and causation

The most consequential warning in this course Do not be deceived by the words "dependent" and "independent" variables. Regression, in its most basic form, cannot prove causality.

The vocabulary is suggestive, and it is meant to be — but "dependent" is a statement about which side of the equation a variable sits on, not about what causes what. Fitting a line establishes association, and association is compatible with X causing Y, Y causing X, some third variable causing both, or coincidence.

So what is regression good for? Testing hypotheses, making predictions, evaluating policies. All of those are valuable, and none of them requires the word "cause". The conditions under which a coefficient can carry a causal reading are the subject of Unit 3B.

4 · The population line and the sample line

Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui is the theoretical specification, and E(Yi | Xi) = β1 + β2Xi is called the population regression line. There is an immediate practical problem with both: we do not usually have information on all the elements in the population. What we usually have is a sample of Y and X values — a random sample of n observations:

{ (xi, yi) : i = 1, …, n }

This is the situation inferential statistics exists for: use sample information to make statements about the population. The quantified version of the theoretical regression equation is the estimated regression equation:

PRFPopulation: E(Yi | X) = β1 + β2Xi
SRFEstimated from a sample: Ŷi = β̂1 + β̂2Xi

The hat "^" indicates an estimated or fitted value. Ŷ is the estimate of Y for a given value of X, built from the estimated intercept β̂1 and estimated slope β̂2. The gap between what was observed and what the line predicts is the residual:

êi = Yi − Ŷi

Disturbance or residual — not the same thing ui is the disturbance: the distance from the observation to the population line. It is unobservable, because the population line is unobservable.
êi is the residual: the distance from the observation to the estimated line. You can compute it, and software will hand you all n of them.
Everything in Unit 2 involves testing assumptions about u using the only evidence available, which is ê.

Estimators and estimates

Definition An estimator is a mathematical technique applied to a sample of data to produce an estimate of the true population regression coefficient.
An estimate is the computed value of a population regression coefficient produced by an estimator.

OLS is an estimator — a recipe. The β̂s it produces are estimates — numbers. Three estimators appear in this course: ordinary least squares, covered here and by far the most widely used; the method of moments; and maximum likelihood, which is taken up in Unit 1E.

5 · How OLS chooses the line

For every fitted value Ŷi there is a residual, and we want the residuals to be as small as possible. For a single-independent-variable model, OLS selects the values of β̂1 and β̂2 that minimise the squared residuals summed over all the sample data points:

min   Σ êi2 = Σ (Yi − Ŷi)2 = Σ (Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi)2

Why squared, and why vertical Residuals are squared because they carry both signs: minimising their plain sum would let a large positive residual cancel a large negative one, and infinitely many terrible lines would score zero. Squaring also penalises one big miss more heavily than several small ones. And the distance being minimised is vertical, not perpendicular, because the model treats X as given and asks how far off it is in predicting Y.

6 · Deriving the estimators

Both routes below start from the same place and arrive at the same answer. Method 1 uses calculus and finishes with matrices; Method 2 stays in ordinary algebra and finishes in deviation form. You should be able to reproduce at least one of them.

Method 1 — calculus, then matrices

  1. Differentiate the sum of squared residuals with respect to β̂1 and set it to zero:

    2 Σ (Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi)(−1) = 0

    Divide by 2 and multiply by −1:

    Σ (Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi) = 0

  2. Apply the summation operator by expanding the brackets, and rearrange:

    Σ Yi − nβ̂1 − β̂2 Σ Xi = 0  ⟹  Σ Yi = nβ̂1 + β̂2 Σ Xi (Equation 1)

  3. Now differentiate with respect to β̂2 and set it to zero:

    2 Σ (Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi)(−Xi) = 0  ⟹  Σ (Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi)(Xi) = 0

  4. Expand and rearrange:

    Σ YiXi − β̂1 Σ Xi − β̂2 Σ Xi2 = 0  ⟹  Σ YiXi = β̂1 Σ Xi + β̂2 Σ Xi2 (Equation 2)

  5. Equations 1 and 2 are two linear equations in two unknowns — the normal equations. In matrix form:

    [ Σ Yi , Σ YiXi ]′ = [ n , Σ Xi ; Σ Xi , Σ Xi2 ] · [ β̂1 , β̂2 ]′

  6. Rearrange by finding the inverse of the 2 × 2 matrix, and solve:

    β̂2 = ( nΣXY − ΣXΣY ) / ( nΣX2 − (ΣX)2 )

    β̂1 = ( ΣX2ΣY − ΣXΣXY ) / ( nΣX2 − (ΣX)2 )

Method 2 — algebra, in deviation form

  1. Start from Equation 1 and divide through by n:

    Ȳ = β̂1 + β̂2X̄  ⟹  β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄

    Note what this says: the fitted line always passes through the point of means (X̄, Ȳ).
  2. Substitute that expression for β̂1 into Equation 2's form Σ(Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi)(Xi) = 0:

    Σ [ Yi − (Ȳ − β̂2X̄) − β̂2Xi ] (Xi) = 0

  3. Expand and factorise:

    Σ [ (Yi − Ȳ) − β̂2(Xi − X̄) ] (Xi) = 0

  4. Following Wooldridge and most texts, write deviations from the mean in lower case — yi = (Yi − Ȳ) and xi = (Xi − X̄):

    Σ [ yi − β̂2xi ] (Xi) = 0  ⟹  Σ yiXi − β̂2 Σ xiXi = 0

  5. Two algebraic identities do the remaining work. Both hold because deviations from the mean sum to zero:

    Σ xiXi = Σ (Xi − X̄)(Xi) = Σ (Xi − X̄)(Xi − X̄) = Σ xi2

    Σ yiXi = Σ (Yi − Ȳ)(Xi) = Σ (Yi − Ȳ)(Xi − X̄) = Σ xiyi

    Why subtracting X̄ from the second factor changes nothing
    Σ(Xi − X̄)X̄ = X̄ Σ(Xi − X̄), and Σ(Xi − X̄) = 0 by the definition of the mean. So subtracting X̄ inside the second bracket subtracts zero overall, and the two expressions are equal. The same argument works for the second identity.
  6. Apply them and solve:

    Σ xiyi − β̂2 Σ xi2 = 0  ⟹  β̂2 = Σ xiyi / Σ xi2 = Σ (Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ) / Σ (Xi − X̄)2

The two forms you should be able to write from memory

β̂2 = Σ (Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ) / Σ (Xi − X̄)2   ·   β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄

The slope is a ratio of the co-movement of X and Y to the variation in X alone. If X does not vary, the denominator is zero and β̂2 does not exist — which is the seed of the multicollinearity problem in Unit 2B.

7 · Assumptions of the simple linear regression model

Before saying anything about the properties of the OLS estimator, some assumptions have to be made. Four are core.

1 Linear in parameters. The regression is linear in the parameters or coefficients, with an additive error term. Note what this does and does not restrict: Y = β1 + β2X2 + u satisfies it, because the parameters enter linearly even though X does not. Y = eβ1Xβ2eu does not.
2 {u1, …, un} and {X1, …, Xn} are independent, which implies E(u | X) = E(u) = 0. The average value of u is zero and is not affected by X.
3 var(ui | Xi) = var(u) = σ2. The error terms have the same variance — homoscedasticity — independent of X. If this fails the variance is non-constant, which is heteroscedasticity (Unit 2C).
4 cov(ui, uj) = 0. No correlation between different error terms. If ui being positive tends to make uj positive, the two disturbances are positively correlated — autocorrelation, very important in time series models (Unit 2D).
Assumption 2 is stronger than it looks Is E(u | X) = 0 the same as saying u and X are uncorrelated? No. Uncorrelated rules out a linear relationship only. E(u | X) = 0 rules out any relationship, linear or non-linear, and is therefore the stronger condition. In practice most people work with the weaker assumption — but know which one you are relying on, and notice that Unit 3B is entirely about what happens when even the weak version fails.

Two further assumptions are sometimes added to the core four:

5Errors are normally distributed: ui ~ N(0, σ2). This is what makes inference about the population parameters — hypothesis testing — possible. Taken up in Unit 1E.
6Fixed regressors: the X values are treated as fixed in repeated sampling rather than random.

8 · Properties of the OLS estimators

The Gauss–Markov theorem If the four core assumptions of the simple linear regression model hold, then the OLS estimators are BLUE.
BBest — the OLS estimator has minimum variance among the class of linear unbiased estimators.
LLinear — β̂ is a linear estimator.
UUnbiased — the expected value of β̂ equals the true value.
EEstimator — β̂ is an estimator of the true value of β.

Unbiasedness and minimum variance are separate virtues, and the difference between them is what the theorem is really about. Imagine three possible sampling distributions of an estimator, all centred somewhere relative to the true β:

AUnbiased, large variance. Centred on the truth, but any single estimate may be far from it.
BUnbiased, small variance. Centred on the truth and tightly concentrated. This is what BLUE promises.
CBiased, medium variance. Tightly concentrated around the wrong answer — the most dangerous of the three, because it looks precise.
Read the theorem carefully Gauss–Markov is conditional: if the assumptions hold, then OLS is BLUE. It says nothing about what OLS is when they fail — and the whole of Unit 2 consists of cases where one of them does. Note also that "best" means best among linear unbiased estimators; a biased or non-linear estimator can beat OLS on other criteria, which is why weighted least squares appears in Unit 2C.

9 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

Explain the difference between the disturbance ui and the residual êi. Which one can you compute, and why does the distinction matter for the second half of this course?
Show solution

ui is the vertical distance from an observation to the population regression line, E(Y | X) = β1 + β2X. It is unobservable, because β1 and β2 are unknown. êi = Yi − Ŷi is the distance to the estimated line, and it is fully computable once β̂1 and β̂2 are in hand.

The distinction matters because every assumption in section 7 is a statement about u — that its mean is zero, its variance constant, its values uncorrelated — while the only evidence available is ê. Every diagnostic test in Unit 2 therefore examines residuals in order to draw a conclusion about disturbances, which is why those tests are indirect and can mislead.

Starting from Σ(Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi) = 0, show that the OLS line necessarily passes through the point of means (X̄, Ȳ).
Show solution

Expanding the summation gives ΣYi − nβ̂1 − β̂2ΣXi = 0. Divide every term by n. Since ΣYi/n = Ȳ and ΣXi/n = X̄, this becomes Ȳ − β̂1 − β̂2X̄ = 0, or Ȳ = β̂1 + β̂2X̄.

That is precisely the statement that the point (X̄, Ȳ) satisfies the equation of the fitted line, so the line passes through it. It also rearranges directly to β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄, which is the formula for the estimated intercept — the two results are the same fact written two ways.

Which of these satisfy Assumption 1? (a) Y = β1 + β2X3 + u; (b) Y = β1 + β22X + u; (c) Y = β1 + β2ln(X) + u; (d) Y = eβ1Xβ2eu. Justify each answer.
Show solution

(a) Yes. The assumption is linearity in the parameters, not in the variables. X3 is just a regressor; define Z = X3 and it is an ordinary simple regression.

(b) No. β2 enters squared, so the equation is non-linear in a parameter.

(c) Yes. Again a transformation of the variable, not of the parameter — and one you will use often, since it turns the slope into an elasticity-style interpretation (Unit 2A Part 2).

(d) Not as written — the parameters enter multiplicatively and through an exponent, and the error is multiplicative rather than additive. Taking logs of both sides gives ln Y = β1 + β2ln X + u, which does satisfy the assumption. This is exactly why logging is such a common first move in applied work.

A colleague reports a regression with a large, highly significant β̂2, and concludes that X causes Y. Separately, they claim their estimator must be reliable "because Gauss–Markov proves OLS is best". Identify what is wrong with each claim.
Show solution

On causation: regression in its most basic form cannot prove causality. A large coefficient establishes association, which is equally consistent with reverse causation, a common cause sitting in u, or coincidence. The labels "dependent" and "independent" describe positions in an equation, not a causal direction.

On Gauss–Markov: the theorem is conditional. It says that if the four core assumptions hold, OLS is BLUE — it makes no claim when they fail, and a colleague who has not tested them has not earned the conclusion. "Best" is also restricted to the class of linear unbiased estimators, so it does not mean no estimator can ever do better. And BLUE concerns the sampling properties of the estimator, not whether the model is correctly specified in the first place.

10 · Check yourself

The sample regression function Ŷi = β̂1 + β̂2Xi differs from the population regression function because
Correct — the PRF is fixed and unobservable; the SRF is computed from whichever sample you drew, and a different sample gives different β̂s.
The PRF is a fixed, unobservable population quantity. The SRF is estimated from one sample, so it varies from sample to sample — which is exactly why we need standard errors in Unit 1F.
OLS minimises the sum of squared residuals rather than their plain sum because
Correct — minimising the plain sum would let big positive and negative misses offset, so infinitely many bad lines would score zero.
Residuals carry both signs, so their plain sum can be driven to zero by lines that fit terribly — the positives simply cancel the negatives. Squaring removes the cancellation and penalises large misses more heavily.
Which model violates the assumption that the regression is linear in parameters?
Correct — the assumption restricts how the parameters enter, and here β2 appears squared. Transforming X is always permitted.
The assumption is linearity in the parameters, not the variables. Squaring, logging or inverting X is fine; a parameter raised to a power is not.
Gauss–Markov tells you that, when the four core assumptions hold, OLS is
Correct — "best" is minimum variance within the class of linear unbiased estimators, not across all estimators.
"Best" is bounded: minimum variance among linear unbiased estimators. A biased estimator may well beat OLS on mean squared error, which is why WLS appears in Unit 2C.
