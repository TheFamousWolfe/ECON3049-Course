Specification: Choice of Explanatory Variables

Omitting a variable that matters, including one that does not, and the cost of each.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • say what specifying an equation consists of, and what a specification error is;
  • derive the omitted variable bias and express it as β3δ̃1;
  • state the two conditions under which the bias vanishes, and sign it in the four cases;
  • explain why the bias does not disappear as n → ∞, and what that makes OLS;
  • say what omitting a variable does to the estimated variance and to hypothesis tests;
  • set out the Ramsey RESET test and explain the intuition behind it;
  • show that including an irrelevant variable is unbiased but inefficient, by the factor 1/(1 − R223);
  • use R̄2, AIC and SC to compare models, and say why theory should outrank all three.
A change of question In everything covered so far we have taken the model as given. The questions were: given a particular model, what is the best way to estimate its parameters? How do we test hypotheses about them? What are the properties of the estimators? All of those require knowledge of the model. This unit asks whether the model is adequate — what matters when specifying it, what the consequences are of getting it wrong, and whether there is any way of assessing it. Unit 2 as a whole is about relaxing the assumptions; this is the first of them to go.

1 · What specification means

Specifying an econometric equation consists of choosing three things:

iThe correct independent variables — this unit.
iiThe correct functional form — Unit 2A Part 2.
iiiThe correct form of the error term — Units 2C and 2D.

Specification error results when any of these choices is made incorrectly. In econometrics we are able to choose which variables to include, and that is a source of both strength and weakness. The strength is obvious. The weakness is that a defensible-looking equation can be wrong in ways the output will not announce.

Two failures are possible, and they are not symmetric. We take underfitting — omitting relevant variables — first, then overfitting.

2 · Underfitting: omitting a relevant variable

To underfit is to leave out variables that belong in the model. The interpretation of the estimated equation becomes suspect immediately, and the first major consequence is possible bias in the coefficients that remain. This is omitted variable bias.

Suppose the true model is

Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui

but you incorrectly assume it is

Yi = β1* + β2*X2i + εi  where  εi = ui + β3X3i

Why the stars, and why they matter The second equation uses β* rather than β because they are not the same parameters. β2 is the partial effect of X2 holding X3 fixed; β2* is whatever the underfit equation is estimating, which includes part of X3's effect. Writing both as β2 hides the entire problem.
Look at what has happened to the error term: the stochastic term now includes the effects of the omitted variable. Everything that follows is a consequence of that one line.

The intuition, before the algebra

From Unit 1C, the simple regression estimator is

β̂2* = Σ x2y / Σ x22

and from Unit 1D, the estimate of β2 from the multiple regression is

β̂2 = [ (Σx2y)(Σx32) − (Σx3y)(Σx2x3) ] / [ (Σx22)(Σx32) − (Σx2x3)2 ]

Clearly the two are not equal, and they would be equal only if Σx2x3 = 0 — that is, only if there is no correlation between X2 and X3. So:

The result in one sentence Omitting a relevant variable leads to biased estimates if there is correlation between the omitted variable and the variables included in the regression. The error term is then no longer independent of the explanatory variable — which violates the classical assumptions directly.

The algebra

  1. Substitute the true model for Y in the formula for β̂2*:

    β̂2* = β2 + β3 ( Σ x2ix3i / Σ x2i2 ) + ( Σ x2iui / Σ x2i2 )

  2. Take expectations. The last term goes to zero, exactly as in Unit 1C — it is the ordinary sampling error. The second term does not:

    E(β̂2*) = β2 + β3 ( Σ x2ix3i / Σ x2i2 )

    That surviving term is a bias in the OLS estimator, caused by estimating the wrong model.
  3. The middle term has a name. Σx2x3/Σx22 is precisely the slope you would get from regressing the omitted variable on the included one — call it δ̃1. So

    Bias(β̂2*) = E(β̂2*) − β2 = β3 δ̃1

The bias exists unless one of two things is true β3 = 0 — X3 has no impact on Y in the true model, so it was never a relevant variable and omitting it costs nothing.
δ̃1 = 0 — no correlation between X2 and X3 in the sample, so X3 sitting in the error term does not move with the regressor.
It is a product. Either factor being zero kills the bias, and neither alone is reassuring.
This bias does not wash out with more data The term β3δ̃1 does not disappear as n → ∞ — δ̃1 converges to a population regression coefficient, not to zero. Omitted variable bias is therefore an asymptotic bias, and the OLS estimators are not merely biased but inconsistent. Compare this with ordinary sampling error, which does vanish with n. Collecting a larger sample is no help whatever here; it just makes you more confident about the wrong number.

Which direction is the bias?

Is it upward — overestimating β2 — or downward? Since the bias is the product β3δ̃1, its sign is just the rule of signs applied to two quantities you can usually reason about from theory even when you cannot measure them.

Generalising: it is not just the correlated coefficient

Consider

Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + β4X4i + ui

but we omit X4i and estimate

Yi = β1* + β2*X2i + β3*X3i + εi

Suppose X2 is correlated with the omitted X4, but X3 is not. Does that mean β̂2* is biased but β̂3* is unbiased? Both are biased.

The general rule Excluding a relevant variable violates the assumption E(u | X) = 0 unless the omitted variable is uncorrelated with ALL of the included independent variables. The bias travels: because X2 and X3 are themselves generally correlated with each other, contamination of one coefficient spreads to the rest. There is no such thing as a locally biased regression.

3 · What omitting a variable does to the variance

Bias is not the only consequence. The variance of the estimator is affected too, and in a way that makes the damage harder to see.

i The variance of the error, σ2, will be incorrectly estimated — the residuals now contain X3's variation as well as u's.
ii It can produce heteroscedasticity. Since E(u | X) ≠ 0 once a variable is omitted, it becomes possible for the variance of the error to change with the explanatory variables — the assumption var(u | X) = σ2 is no longer protected.
iii The conventionally measured variances of the OLS estimators are therefore biased, and so are the standard errors — which are just their square roots.

Comparing the two formulas already derived:

underfit: var(β̂2*) = σ2 / Σx22  ·  correct: var(β̂2) = σ2 / [ Σx22 ( 1 − R223 ) ]

where R223 is the R2 from regressing X2 on X3. When are they equal? Only when R223 = 0 — the same uncorrelated-regressors condition as before. Otherwise the underfit model reports the smaller variance, since it is missing the (1 − R223) factor in the denominator.

The two errors point the same way, which is the dangerous part Omitting a relevant correlated variable biases the coefficient and understates its standard error. The result is a coefficient that is wrong and looks more precise than it is — so hypothesis-testing procedures are likely to give misleading conclusions about statistical significance. A misspecified model does not announce itself with wide confidence intervals; it announces itself with narrow ones around the wrong answer.

4 · Worked example: family income and education

Consider a sample of married couples where both husband and wife work. The dependent variable is annual family income (FAMINC), the combined income of the two. We are interested in the impact of education — both the husband's years (HEDUC) and the wife's (WEDUC):

FAMINC = β1 + β2HEDUC + β3WEDUC + u

Suppose we estimate the equation omitting WEDUC. Would this lead to biased coefficients and biased standard errors, and in which direction?

Reasoning it out before looking Bias = β3δ̃1. We do not know β3, since it is an unknown population parameter — but we can state an a-priori expectation: β3 > 0, because a wife's education should raise family income. For δ̃1, the slope of WEDUC on HEDUC, assortative mating gives δ̃1 > 0 — more educated people tend to marry each other. Two positives, so by Table 1 the bias is upward: omitting WEDUC should overstate the return to the husband's education.
If we had data on the omitted variable we could estimate δ̃1 directly. If not — the usual case — the a-priori argument is what we have, and it is often enough to sign the bias.

The auxiliary regression, WEDUC on HEDUC:

WEDUĈ =s.e.t = 6.5167(0.2800)23.27 + 0.4619 HEDUC(0.0218)21.19 R2 = 0.3748  ·  so δ̃1 = 0.4619, positive as expected

Omitting WEDUC:

FAMINĈ =s.e.t = −26191(8541)−3.07 + 5155 HEDUC(658)7.83

Including WEDUC:

FAMINĈ =s.e.t = −5534(11230)−0.49 + 3132 HEDUC(803)3.90 + 4523 WEDUC(1066)4.24
Both predictions come true The coefficient. 5155 against 3132 — the underfit model overstates the return to the husband's education by about 2000, an upward bias exactly as Table 1 predicted. The prediction can even be checked numerically: β3δ̃1 ≈ 4523 × 0.4619 ≈ 2089, and 3132 + 2089 = 5221, close to the 5155 actually estimated.
The standard error. 658 against 803 — the underfit model reports the smaller standard error, making its inflated coefficient look more precisely estimated than the correct one. That is the (1 − R223) factor, with R223 = 0.3748, doing exactly what section 3 said it would.

5 · Detecting and correcting it

In theory the solution seems easy: add the omitted variable to the equation. Unfortunately this is difficult for a few reasons — omitted variable bias is hard to detect, and once you suspect it, which variable do you add?

Informal indicators

i The model does not match its theoretical underpinnings. Do you have all the variables theory suggests?
ii A significant coefficient with the wrong sign — significant in the direction opposite to what was expected.
iii Patterns in the residuals. If a variable is missing we are likely to see structure in the residuals, so residual plots are worth making routinely.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It plots the residuals of an underfit model against the omitted variable, showing a clear trend, and a control that puts the variable back into the model and removes the pattern.

These indicators are informal, and usually not the best available. The formal method is the Ramsey RESET.

The Ramsey RESET test

Ramsey proposed a general test of specification error called RESET — REgression Specification Error Test. Testing for misspecification is a way of asking whether our model is adequate, or whether we can improve on it. RESET is designed to detect omitted variables and incorrect functional form, and it returns in Unit 2A Part 2 for the second of those.

The hypotheses H0: the model is correctly specified — no specification error.
H1: the model is not correctly specified — there is specification error.
  1. Specify and estimate the model — call it Equation 1:

    Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + β4X4i + ui

    Ŷi = β̂1 + β̂2X2i + β̂3X3i + β̂4X4i

    and save the fitted values Ŷ.
  2. Re-specify Equation 1, introducing Ŷ in some non-linear form. It is popular to add Ŷ2, or both Ŷ2 and Ŷ3; we will be general and include both:

    Yi = α1 + α2X2i + α3X3i + α4X4i + γ1Ŷi2 + γ2Ŷi3 + ui

  3. Test whether the additional regressors are jointly zero:

    H0: γ1 = γ2 = 0  ·  H1: γ1 ≠ 0 or γ2 ≠ 0

    Which should look familiar — it is an F-test of exclusion restrictions, exactly as in Unit 1F, with q = 2.
Why powers of Ŷ can stand in for variables you do not have Ŷ is a linear combination of the Xs, so adding Ŷ2 and Ŷ3 is akin to adding non-linear functions of them — X22, X2X3, X32 and so on — all at once, at a cost of only two degrees of freedom. If there are omitted variables correlated with the included Xs, they will also be correlated with those non-linear terms, and their impact gets picked up.
So: if we can significantly improve the model by artificially including powers of its own predictions, the original model must have been inadequate. Note what this does and does not give you — a rejection says something is wrong, not what to add.

6 · Overfitting: including an irrelevant variable

The consequences of omitting relevant variables may suggest that a good strategy is to include as many variables as possible. It is not — though the reason is different from what you might expect, and much milder.

Suppose the true model is Y = β1 + β2X2 + u, but we estimate

Y = α1 + α2X2 + α3X3 + ε

The good news It does not matter whether X2 and X3 are correlated. The OLS estimates from the "incorrect" model are unbiased and consistent: E(α̂1) = β1, E(α̂2) = β2, and E(α̂3) = β3 = 0. Overfitting and underfitting are not mirror images: one destroys unbiasedness, the other does not.

So what is the problem? The inclusion of an irrelevant variable increases the variance of the estimated coefficients. The α̂s are generally inefficient — their variances are larger than those of the β̂s from the true model:

var(β̂2) = σ2 / Σx22  ·  var(α̂2) = σ2 / [ Σx22 ( 1 − R223 ) ]

⟹ var(α̂2) / var(β̂2) = 1 / ( 1 − R223 )

Since 0 ≤ R223 ≤ 1, it follows that var(α̂2) ≥ var(β̂2), with equality only when the irrelevant variable is uncorrelated with the relevant one.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It compares the sampling distributions of the slope on X₂ from the correct model and from a model overfitted with an irrelevant regressor; both are centred on the true value, and the overfitted one widens as the correlation between the two regressors is increased.

The asymmetry worth remembering Omitting a relevant variable: biased, inconsistent, and the standard errors are wrong too. Fatal.
Including an irrelevant one: unbiased and consistent, but inefficient — wider sampling distributions, larger standard errors, smaller t-statistics, real effects you can no longer detect.
When genuinely unsure and the variable is plausible on theory, the asymmetry argues for including it. That is not a licence to include everything.

7 · Choosing the set of regressors

Testing

Suppose we have a k-variable model to explain some phenomenon, and we are not sure whether Xk belongs — theory gives little guidance. One simple solution is to test its statistical significance: a two-sided test of H0: βk = 0. If we are unsure whether X3, X4 and X5 legitimately belong, an F-test of exclusion restrictions does the same job for the three together.

Model selection criteria

Researchers also compare models using R̄2, the Akaike Information Criterion and the Schwarz Criterion. All three trade goodness of fit against the number of regressors; they differ in how heavily they charge for the second.

The three criteria

R̄2 = 1 − [ RSS/(n − k) ] / [ TSS/(n − 1) ]

AIC = n · ln( RSS/n ) + 2v

SC = ln( RSS/n ) + ( v/n ) ln n

where v is the number of variables and n the sample size. In each case the first term falls as extra variables are added, reflecting the decline in RSS, while the second rises because v does. SC penalises extra variables more heavily than AIC, so it selects more parsimonious models. In both cases, the model with the smallest value is preferred.
R̄2 is not perfect It loses the R2 interpretation — it is no longer the proportion of variation explained, and it can be negative.
More seriously, adding a variable Xk raises R̄2 whenever the t-statistic for H0: βk = 0 exceeds 1 in absolute value. A t of 1.40 is nowhere near significant at 5% or even 10%, yet R̄2 will still rise. Selecting on R̄2 is therefore a much weaker filter than testing — closer to a 30% significance level than a 5% one.

The advice that actually matters

i It is not a "sin" to have insignificant variables in your regression. Finding a variable insignificant is informative, and there is no reason to hide it by re-estimating without it.
ii But do not include so many variables that you end up with multicollinearity — a sample too small for the number of parameters being estimated. That is Unit 2B.
iii These methods should not be applied blindly. They earn their place mainly in forecasting, or where the sample is small and degrees of freedom must be conserved.
iv Select on theoretical grounds, not statistical ones. Include explanatory variables that directly influence the dependent variable on theory, and whose effects are not already accounted for by other included variables.

General-to-specific modelling

If statistical criteria are used to specify a model, the direction of travel matters. It is better not to use a bottom-up approach — starting with a small model and expanding — because every test along the way is conducted on a model already known to be misspecified.

The alternative is the general-to-specific approach, also known as the LSE methodology, advocated by David Hendry and others. It starts by estimating a general unrestricted model (GUM), which is subsequently reduced in size and complexity by testing restrictions that can be imposed.

The argument for it Assuming a sufficiently general and complicated model can describe reality, any more parsimonious model is an improvement if it conveys all of the same information in a simpler, more compact form. Starting general and testing down means each restriction is tested within a model that is, so far as you know, adequately specified — which is exactly the condition every test in Unit 1F assumed.

8 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

Starting from the true model Y = β1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + u, derive E(β̂2*) for the underfit regression of Y on X2 alone, and state the two conditions under which the estimator remains unbiased.
Show solution

The underfit estimator is β̂2* = Σx2y/Σx22. Substituting the true model for Y and using Σx2 = 0:

β̂2* = β2 + β3(Σx2x3/Σx22) + (Σx2u/Σx22)

Taking expectations, the last term vanishes because E(ui) = 0 and the x2 are fixed. The middle term survives:

E(β̂2*) = β2 + β3δ̃1,   δ̃1 = Σx2x3/Σx22

Unbiased if either factor is zero: β3 = 0, meaning X3 does not belong in the true model at all, so nothing relevant was omitted; or δ̃1 = 0, meaning X2 and X3 are uncorrelated in the sample, so the omitted variable sitting in the error term does not move with the regressor.

Note that δ̃1 is the slope of the auxiliary regression of the omitted variable on the included one — a quantity you can sometimes estimate, and can almost always sign from theory.

A researcher regresses house prices on floor area, omitting distance to the city centre. Larger houses tend to be further out, and being further out lowers prices. Sign the bias in the coefficient on floor area, and say what happens to it as the sample grows.
Show solution

Sign the two factors. The omitted variable is distance, and its effect on price is negative, so β3 < 0. The auxiliary regression of distance on floor area has a positive slope, since larger houses tend to be further out, so δ̃1 > 0.

The bias is β3δ̃1 < 0 — a downward bias. This is the bottom-left cell of Table 1. The estimated return to floor area understates the true partial effect, because part of what the regression attributes to size is really the price penalty of the outlying location that tends to come with it.

As n grows: nothing improves. δ̃1 converges to a population regression coefficient, not to zero, so the bias term does not shrink. Omitted variable bias is an asymptotic bias, and the estimator is inconsistent — a larger sample simply narrows the confidence interval around the wrong number.

Using the FAMINC results in section 4, verify the predicted bias numerically, and explain why the standard error on HEDUC is smaller in the misspecified equation. Why is that combination particularly dangerous?
Show solution

The bias. β̂3 = 4523 and δ̃1 = 0.4619, so the predicted bias is 4523 × 0.4619 ≈ 2089. Adding that to the correct estimate: 3132 + 2089 = 5221, against the 5155 actually obtained from the underfit regression. The small gap is sampling error — the relationship Bias = β3δ̃1 is exact in expectation, not in any one sample.

The standard error. The correct model's variance carries a factor 1/(1 − R223) that the underfit model does not. With R223 = 0.3748, that factor is 1/0.6252 = 1.60, so the correct standard error is larger by roughly √1.60 = 1.26 — and indeed 658 × 1.26 ≈ 829, close to the 803 reported.

Why the combination is dangerous. The two errors point the same way. The coefficient is inflated and its standard error is understated, so its t-statistic is doubly overstated: 7.83 in the misspecified equation against 3.90 in the correct one. A misspecified model here does not look uncertain — it looks better than the truth. Nothing in its own output would lead you to suspect it.

A colleague argues: "Since omitting a relevant variable is fatal and including an irrelevant one is merely inefficient, always include everything you can measure." Evaluate this, and say when you would and would not follow it.
Show solution

The argument has real force. The asymmetry is genuine: omitting a relevant correlated variable gives bias that does not vanish with n and standard errors that mislead, while including an irrelevant one leaves the estimates unbiased and consistent and costs only precision, by the factor 1/(1 − R223). Faced with a plausible variable and genuine uncertainty, including it is the safer error.

Where it breaks down. Efficiency is not free. As irrelevant variables accumulate, standard errors inflate, t-statistics fall, and real effects become undetectable — you have traded a wrong answer for no answer. Degrees of freedom are consumed, which matters acutely in the small samples typical of macroeconomic work. Enough correlated regressors produces multicollinearity outright (Unit 2B). And "can measure" is not "should include": a variable that is itself caused by Y, or that lies on the causal path between X2 and Y, does damage of a different kind that no amount of unbiasedness protects against.

The working rule is the deck's: select on theoretical grounds, not statistical ones. Include variables that directly influence Y and whose effects are not already captured by something else in the model. Where theory is silent and the sample is generous, lean towards inclusion; where the sample is small, be sparing, and prefer the general-to-specific route.

Explain how the RESET test can detect an omitted variable that the researcher does not have data on. What are the limits of what a rejection tells you?
Show solution

How it works. Ŷ is a linear combination of the included Xs, so Ŷ2 and Ŷ3 contain squares and cross-products of them — X22, X2X3 and so on — bundled into two regressors. If a relevant variable has been omitted and is correlated with the included Xs, it will also be correlated with those non-linear combinations, so some of its effect can be picked up by γ1 and γ2. An F-test of H0: γ1 = γ2 = 0 then rejects. The appeal is that this needs no data on the omitted variable at all — only the model's own fitted values.

The limits. A rejection says the model is inadequate; it does not say why, and certainly not which variable to add. RESET is equally sensitive to incorrect functional form (Unit 2A Part 2), so a rejection leaves at least two very different diagnoses open. It has no power against an omitted variable uncorrelated with the included regressors — but such a variable causes no bias anyway, so little is lost. And failing to reject is weak evidence: it means this particular test found nothing, not that the model is correct.
