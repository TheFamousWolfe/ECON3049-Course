The Normality Assumption and Maximum Likelihood

Adding a distribution to the error term, and a second route to the same estimates.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • explain why estimation alone is not enough, and what a distribution for u buys;
  • state the normality assumption, and the difference between the CLRM and the CNLRM;
  • give the four arguments for assuming normality, and say what the CLT argument requires;
  • write down the sampling distributions of β̂1, β̂2 and σ̂2 under normality;
  • say what BUE adds to BLUE, and why Rao's result is stronger than Gauss–Markov;
  • derive the likelihood function, take logs, and obtain the ML estimators;
  • show that the ML βs equal the OLS βs, and that σ̃2 is biased while σ̂2 is not.
Why this unit exists The classical theory of statistical inference has two branches: estimation and hypothesis testing. Units 1B to 1D did estimation — and showed, using the classical assumptions, that OLS is BLUE. But we are still not in a position to say how close an estimate is to the true parameter. The estimates come from a sample and are random variables; to make probability statements about them we need to know their distribution. That is what this unit supplies, and Unit 1F is what it is for.

1 · Where the distribution of β̂2 comes from

Recall from Unit 1C that the OLS slope is a weighted average of the Y values, with weights ki = xi/Σxi2:

β̂2 = Σ kiYi

β̂2 is a linear function of Y because the Xs are fixed by assumption. Substituting Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui and using the properties of the weights:

β̂2 = β2 + Σ kiui

The whole unit follows from this line Since the ki, the βs and the Xs are all fixed by assumption, β̂2 is a linear function of the stochastic error term ui and of nothing else. So the probability distribution of β̂2 depends entirely on the assumptions we are willing to make about the probability distribution of ui. Until now we have assumed only its mean and variance. To get a whole distribution out, a whole distribution has to go in.

2 · The normality assumption

We assume that ui is normally distributed, which is quite common in the regression context:

E(ui) = 0  ·  var(ui) = σ2  ·  cov(ui, uj) = 0 for i ≠ j

which compactly is

ui ~ N(0, σ2)

where "~" stands for "distributed as" and N for the normal distribution; the terms in the parentheses are the mean and the variance. If the covariance between two normally distributed random variables is zero, the two variables are independent. So with cov(ui, uj) = 0 for i ≠ j, the disturbances are normally and independently distributed:

ui ~ NID(0, σ2)

CLRM and CNLRM The classical linear regression model (CLRM) is the set of assumptions from Units 1C and 1D. Adding the normality assumption to it gives the classical normal linear regression model (CNLRM). Nothing already proved is lost — OLS is still BLUE — but a great deal is gained, because the estimators now have known distributions.
Note carefully that zero covariance implies independence only because the variables are normal. In general, zero covariance rules out linear association and nothing more; this is one of the places where the normality assumption is quietly doing work.

3 · Why assume normality?

i The central limit theorem. The disturbance represents the total influence on Y of a large number of variables not explicitly in the model, each small and random. The CLT says that if there are a large number of independent and identically distributed random variables, then with few exceptions the distribution of their sum tends to normal as the number of variables increases indefinitely. Even where the number is not large or they are not quite independent, their sum may still be approximately normal.
ii Any linear function of normal variables is itself normal. The OLS estimators are linear functions of ui, so normality of the disturbances gives normality of the estimators. This is the property that makes the assumption worth having at all.
iii It is simple and it is common. The normal distribution has well-known theoretical properties, is described entirely by two parameters — mean and variance — and turns up in a great many real-world phenomena.
iv It matters most in small samples. With n small or finite (under about 100) normality is what lets us derive the distributions of the estimators and use the t, F and χ2 tests. In large samples the t and F statistics have approximately those distributions anyway, so the tests remain valid even without it.

What the CLT argument does not cover The theorem needs the influences to be numerous, independent and individually small. If one omitted factor dominates all the others — a policy change, a crisis, a single missing regressor with a large effect — the sum is that factor plus noise, and there is no reason to expect it to be normal. That is not a reason to distrust the assumption in general; it is a reason to check residuals rather than assume, and to know that a badly non-normal residual plot is usually telling you about a specification problem rather than a distributional one.

4 · Properties of OLS under normality

1They are unbiased.
2They have minimum variance. Combined with the first, they are minimum-variance unbiased, or efficient, estimators.
3They are consistent: as the sample size increases indefinitely, the estimators converge to their true population values.

The sampling distributions

Because β̂1 and β̂2 are linear functions of a normal variable, they are themselves normal, with the means and variances established in Unit 1C:

The four results to memorise

β̂1 ~ N( β1, σβ̂12 )  where  σβ̂12 = σ2 ΣXi2 / (n Σxi2)

β̂2 ~ N( β2, σβ̂22 )  where  σβ̂22 = σ2 / Σxi2

Z = ( β̂2 − β2 ) / σβ̂2 ~ N(0, 1)  and likewise for β̂1

( n − 2 ) σ̂2 / σ2 ~ χ2(n−2)

The standardisation in the third line is the familiar one from first-year statistics — subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. It is the single most important step in this unit, because the moment an estimator can be standardised to N(0, 1), probability statements about it become possible, and a probability statement about a coefficient is exactly what a hypothesis test is.

Two further results complete the picture:

i β̂1 and β̂2 are distributed independently of σ̂2. This matters in Unit 1F: it is what allows a normal numerator and a chi-square denominator to be combined into a t-distribution.
ii With ui ~ N(0, σ2) and Yi a linear function of ui, Yi is normally distributed too: Yi ~ N( β1 + β2Xi, σ2 ).
BUE — a stronger claim than BLUE Under normality, β̂1 and β̂2 have minimum variance in the entire class of unbiased estimators, whether linear or not. This result, due to Rao, is very powerful because — unlike the Gauss–Markov theorem — it is not restricted to linear estimators. The least-squares estimators are therefore best unbiased estimators (BUE).
Compare the two carefully. Gauss–Markov needs no distributional assumption but only ranks OLS against linear rivals. Rao's result costs one extra assumption — normality — and in exchange rules out every unbiased rival there is.

5 · Maximum likelihood

Most of this course has used OLS. Maximum likelihood (ML) is a second estimation method with some stronger theoretical properties. The headline result, which the rest of this section proves, is worth stating first:

The result If the ui are normally distributed, the ML and OLS estimators of the regression coefficients — the βs — are identical, for both the simple and the multiple regression. The ML estimator of σ2, however, is σ̃2 = Σûi2/n, which is biased, unlike the OLS estimator σ̂2 = Σûi2/(n − 2). As n grows the two coincide, so the ML estimator is asymptotically unbiased.

The derivation

Take the two-variable model Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui, where the Yi are normally and independently distributed with mean β1 + β2Xi and variance σ2.

  1. The joint probability density of the sample, given the mean and variance (Equation 1). Because the Ys are independent, the joint density is the product of the individual densities:

    f(Y1, …, Yn) = f(Y1) f(Y2) ⋯ f(Yn)

  2. Each individual density is the normal density (Equation 2):

    f(Yi) = ( 1 / σ√(2π) ) · exp[ − ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )2 / 2σ2 ]

  3. Substituting Equation 2 into Equation 1 for each observation (Equation 3):

    f(Y1, …, Yn) = [ 1 / σn(√(2π))n ] · exp[ − ½ Σ ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )2 / σ2 ]

  4. Now read the same expression backwards. If the Ys are known and β1, β2 and σ2 are unknown, Equation 3 is the likelihood function (Equation 4):

    LF(β1, β2, σ2) = [ 1 / σn(√(2π))n ] · exp[ − ½ Σ ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )2 / σ2 ]

    The idea in one sentence The method of maximum likelihood consists of estimating the unknown parameters in such a manner that the probability of observing the given Ys is as high as possible. The same formula is a density when the parameters are known and the data is not, and a likelihood when the data is known and the parameters are not.
  5. Maximising a product is awkward, so take natural logs. Since the log is a monotonic function, ln LF attains its maximum at the same point as LF (Equation 5):

    ln LF = − (n/2) ln σ2 − (n/2) ln(2π) − ½ Σ ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )2 / σ2

  6. Differentiate partially with respect to β1, β2 and σ2 (Equations 6, 7, 8):

    ∂lnLF/∂β1 = − (1/σ2) Σ ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )(−1)

    ∂lnLF/∂β2 = − (1/σ2) Σ ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )(−Xi)

    ∂lnLF/∂σ2 = − n/2σ2 + (1/2σ4) Σ ( Yi − β1 − β2Xi )2

  7. Set each to zero — the first-order conditions — and write β̃1, β̃2, σ̃2 for the ML estimators (Equations 9, 10). After simplifying, the first two give (Equations 11, 12):

    Σ Yi = n β̃1 + β̃2 Σ Xi

    Σ YiXi = β̃1 Σ Xi + β̃2 Σ Xi2

Look at what those two equations are They are precisely the normal equations of least-squares theory from Unit 1B. So the ML estimators β̃ are the same as the OLS estimators β̂.
The equality is not accidental. Examine the log-likelihood in Equation 5: the last term is the sum of squared deviations, and it enters with a negative sign. Making ln LF as large as possible therefore means making that sum as small as possible — which is exactly the least-squares criterion.

Where the two methods disagree

  1. Substituting the ML estimators into the third first-order condition and simplifying gives the ML estimator of the variance (Equation 13):

    σ̃2 = (1/n) Σ ( Yi − β̃1 − β̃2Xi )2 = (1/n) Σ ( Yi − β̂1 − β̂2Xi )2 = (1/n) Σ ûi2

  2. This differs from the OLS estimator σ̂2 = Σûi2/(n − 2), which Unit 1C showed to be unbiased. Taking expectations of Equation 13, and using E(Σûi2) = (n − 2)σ2 from that same derivation (Equation 14):

    E(σ̃2) = (1/n) E( Σ ûi2 ) = ( (n − 2)/n ) σ2 = σ2 − (2/n) σ2

  3. So σ̃2 is biased downward in small samples, by exactly 2σ2/n. As n increases indefinitely the bias term vanishes, so σ̃2 is asymptotically unbiased: lim E(σ̃2) = σ2 as n → ∞. It can further be shown to be consistent.
Why the downward bias is the dangerous direction A variance estimate that is too small makes every standard error too small, every t-statistic too large, and every coefficient look more significant than the evidence supports. With n = 10 the ML estimator understates σ2 by 20%. This is the same arithmetic as the n versus n − 2 argument in Unit 1C, seen from the other side — and it is why software reports the OLS variance estimate even when it has fitted by maximum likelihood.

6 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

Explain why assuming a distribution for ui is enough to determine the distribution of β̂2. Which earlier assumption does the argument rely on, and what would go wrong without it?
Show solution

From Unit 1C, β̂2 = β2 + Σkiui. The term β2 is a fixed population constant and the ki are constants too, so the only random object on the right-hand side is u. β̂2 is therefore a linear function of the disturbances, and a linear function of normal variables is itself normal — with mean β2 by unbiasedness and variance σ2/Σxi2.

The assumption relied on is assumption 2, that the Xs are fixed or non-stochastic. Without it the ki = xi/Σxi2 are themselves random, β̂2 becomes a ratio of random quantities rather than a weighted sum with fixed weights, and no amount of knowledge about the distribution of u alone determines its distribution.

A student writes: "cov(ui, uj) = 0 means the disturbances are independent." Under the CLRM of Unit 1C, is this correct? Under the CNLRM? Explain the difference.
Show solution

Under the CLRM, no. Zero covariance rules out a linear association between ui and uj and nothing more. Two variables can be uncorrelated and still strongly dependent — for example if uj is a deterministic function of ui2.

Under the CNLRM, yes. For variables that are jointly normally distributed, zero covariance does imply independence; this is a special property of the normal distribution, not a general fact. So the statement becomes true only once normality has been assumed, and the disturbances can then be described as normally and independently distributed, NID(0, σ2).

The general lesson is worth carrying into Unit 2: an assumption often does more work than the line it appears on, and it is worth asking which results would survive without it. Unbiasedness would; independence would not.

Show that maximising the log-likelihood with respect to β1 and β2 is the same problem as minimising Σûi2. Then explain why the two methods nonetheless produce different estimators of σ2.
Show solution

The log-likelihood is ln LF = −(n/2)ln σ2 − (n/2)ln(2π) − ½Σ(Yi − β1 − β2Xi)2/σ2. For fixed σ2, the first two terms do not involve β1 or β2 at all. The third is the sum of squared deviations multiplied by the negative constant −1/2σ2. Maximising a negative constant times a quantity is minimising the quantity — so the β that maximises ln LF is exactly the β that minimises Σ(Yi − β1 − β2Xi)2. Setting the derivatives to zero produces the least-squares normal equations, as in section 5.

Why σ2 differs. The two methods answer different questions about it. ML treats σ2 as one more parameter to be chosen to maximise the likelihood, and the answer to that optimisation is Σûi2/n — it makes no correction for the fact that two parameters have already been estimated from the same data. OLS chooses the divisor n − 2 specifically so that E(σ̂2) = σ2. Unbiasedness is a property ML never promised: what ML guarantees is consistency and asymptotic efficiency, which σ̃2 does have.

With n = 12, by what percentage does the ML estimator of σ2 understate the true value on average? What happens to a t-statistic computed with it, and at what sample size does the discrepancy fall below 2%?
Show solution

E(σ̃2) = ((n − 2)/n)σ2 = (10/12)σ2 = 0.833σ2, so it understates σ2 by 16.7% on average.

Effect on a t-statistic. The standard error is the square root of a variance built from σ̃2, so it is understated by a factor √0.833 = 0.913 — about 8.7% too small. Since t = β̂/se, the t-statistic is inflated by roughly 1/0.913 = 1.095, about 9.5% too large. A coefficient with a true t of 1.90 would be reported at about 2.08 and declared significant at 5% when it should not be.

Below 2%: the bias factor is 2/n, so 2/n < 0.02 requires n > 100. This is the arithmetic behind the deck's remark that normality and these small-sample corrections matter most below about 100 observations.
