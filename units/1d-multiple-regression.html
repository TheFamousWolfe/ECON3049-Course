Multiple Regression

Holding other things equal — several regressors at once, and what a partial slope means.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • say why the two-variable model is almost never an adequate representation of economic reality;
  • read the three-variable model in the course's subscript notation, and interpret β2 as a partial coefficient;
  • state the nine assumptions of the multiple regression model, and say which three are new;
  • derive the normal equations and write β̂1, β̂2 and β̂3 in deviation form;
  • state the variances and covariance of the partial slopes, and why the divisor of σ̂2 is now n − 3;
  • show that the regression surface passes through the point of means and that Ȳ̂ = Ȳ;
  • derive R2, explain why it never falls when a regressor is added, and use adjusted R2 instead.
Where we are Units 1B and 1C derived the OLS estimators for the two-variable model, established their properties under the classical assumptions, calculated their variances, and measured goodness of fit. Every one of those results is about to be done again with one more regressor. Almost nothing new happens conceptually — but the notation changes, one assumption is added, and one measure of fit stops working.

2 · Why multiple regression

There is nearly never a case where the two-variable model is an adequate representation of economic reality. By specifying consumption as a function of income alone, we are implicitly assuming that income is the only important factor driving consumption.

That is not the case. Other known factors — wealth, prices — can be important determinants of consumption. The two-variable model assumes, unrealistically, that all of them have no statistical impact on consumption and that they are uncorrelated with income. The second half of that sentence is the dangerous one, and section 3 of this unit is about what it costs.

iIncorporate more explanatory factors into the model.
iiExplicitly hold fixed other factors that would otherwise be in the error term.
iiiAllow for more flexible functional forms.

Two examples

wage = β1 + β2 education + β3 experience + u

Here wage is the hourly wage, education is years of education, experience is years of experience, and u is everything else affecting the average hourly wage. β2 measures the impact of education on the hourly wage holding experience constant, and β3 measures the impact of experience holding education constant. That phrase is what the whole unit is buying.

average score = β1 + β2 student_spend + β3 average_income + u

Per-student spending is likely to be correlated with average family income at a given school, because of how schools are financed. Omitting average family income would lead to a biased estimate of the effect of spending on test scores: in a simple regression, the effect attributed to spending would partly include the effect of family income on scores. 3 · The three-variable model

Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui

As in the two-variable case, Yi is the dependent variable, β1 is the intercept and ui is the stochastic error term. What is new is that there are now two independent variables, X2i and X3i, whose impact on Yi is measured by the partial coefficients β2 and β3.

Note the change in notation There are only two explanatory variables, but they are numbered 2 and 3, not 1 and 2. The subscripts on the Xs are chosen so that each variable carries the same number as its own parameter: β2 goes with X2i, β3 with X3i. The intercept keeps β1 and has no X. Get this straight now — every formula in this unit and in Unit 2 depends on it.

Taking conditional expectations, and using E(ui | X2i, X3i) = 0:

E(Yi | X2i, X3i) = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i

The interpretation is exactly as in the two-variable case: the model gives the conditional mean of Y given fixed values of X2i and X3i.

Partial coefficient β2 and β3 are called partial coefficients because each represents the partial or net effect of its own variable on Y, holding the other variable constant — ceteris paribus. Formally, β2 measures the change in the mean value of Y per unit change in X2, with X3 held constant, and symmetrically for β3. This is why a regression coefficient reported from a multiple regression must always be read together with the list of what else was in the model.

Generalising, and a warning about k

The three-variable model is used here because the algebra stays readable. Everything generalises to the k-variable case:

Ŷi = β̂1 + β̂2X2i + β̂3X3i + … + β̂kXki + ûi

k means different things in your two textbooks In this course, following Gujarati and Porter, k is the number of unknown parameters including the intercept. A three-variable model has k = 3. Wooldridge writes the same model with k + 1 parameters, where k counts only the slopes. Both are internally consistent; they give different-looking degrees of freedom for the same model, so check which book a formula came from before using its n − k.

4 · The assumptions, restated

We work in the same framework as Unit 1C. Two of the nine below are refinements of assumption 7, and one — the last — is new:

1The regression model is linear in parameters.
2The values of the independent variables are fixed / non-stochastic.
3No relationship between the regressors and the disturbance: cov(X2i, ui) = cov(X3i, ui) = 0.
4The disturbance has a mean of zero: E(ui | X2i, X3i) = 0.
5Homoscedasticity: var(ui) = σ2.
6No autocorrelation: cov(ui, uj) = 0 for i ≠ j.
7There must be variation in the independent variables.
8No perfect collinearity between the explanatory variables.
9No specification bias — the model is correctly specified.
7, 8 and 9 read off the formulas Assumptions 7 and 8 are not decoration: the denominator of every partial slope below is Σx2i2Σx3i2 − (Σx2ix3i)2. With no variation in a regressor, or with one regressor an exact multiple of another, that denominator is zero and the estimators simply do not exist. Assumption 9 is the one the figure in section 2 was about — and it is the subject of Unit 2A.

5 · Estimating the model

  1. Write the sample regression function corresponding to the PRF:

    Yi = β̂1 + β̂2X2i + β̂3X3i + ûi

    where ûi is the residual, the sample counterpart of ui.
  2. Exactly as in the two-variable case, choose the unknown parameters to minimise the sum of squared residuals:

    min   Σ ûi2 = Σ (Yi − β̂1 − β̂2X2i − β̂3X3i)2

  3. Differentiate with respect to each unknown parameter in turn and set each derivative to zero. Three parameters give three normal equations:

    Σ Yi = n β̂1 + β̂2 Σ X2i + β̂3 Σ X3i

    Σ YiX2i = β̂1 Σ X2i + β̂2 Σ X2i2 + β̂3 Σ X2iX3i

    Σ YiX3i = β̂1 Σ X3i + β̂2 Σ X2iX3i + β̂3 Σ X3i2

  4. The first normal equation, divided by n, gives the intercept immediately — and says that the fitted surface passes through the point of means:

    β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄2 − β̂3X̄3

  5. Writing the remaining two in deviation form and solving the pair simultaneously:

    β̂2 = [ (Σx2iyi)(Σx3i2) − (Σx3iyi)(Σx2ix3i) ] / [ (Σx2i2)(Σx3i2) − (Σx2ix3i)2 ]

    β̂3 = [ (Σx3iyi)(Σx2i2) − (Σx2iyi)(Σx2ix3i) ] / [ (Σx2i2)(Σx3i2) − (Σx2ix3i)2 ]

Two things to notice, both examinable They are symmetrical. One is obtained from the other by interchanging the roles of X2 and X3. If you can write one you can write both.
The denominators are identical, and the three-variable case is a natural extension of the two-variable one: set Σx2ix3i = 0 — the regressors uncorrelated — and β̂2 collapses straight back to Σx2iyi/Σx2i2, the Unit 1C formula. Correlation between the regressors is the only thing that makes multiple regression different arithmetic, and when it becomes severe it is Unit 2B.

6 · Variances, and the new degrees of freedom

Having obtained the estimators, their variances follow by the same route as Unit 1C. Write D for the common denominator, D = Σx2i2Σx3i2 − (Σx2ix3i)2:

The variance and covariance formulas

var(β̂2) = σ2 Σx3i2 / D  ·  var(β̂3) = σ2 Σx2i2 / D

cov(β̂2, β̂3) = − σ2 Σx2ix3i / D

var(β̂1) = σ2 [ 1/n + ( X̄22Σx3i2 + X̄32Σx2i2 − 2X̄2X̄3Σx2ix3i ) / D ]

The standard error of each is the square root of its variance, with σ2 replaced by σ̂2.

Two observations carry over unchanged from Unit 1C. The variance and covariance of the estimated parameters still rely on the unknown population variance σ2, so it still has to be estimated. And there is still a positive relationship between the variance of the error term and the variance of the model parameters — noisier data, less precise estimates.

A third observation is new, and it is the seed of Unit 2B. As the two regressors become more strongly correlated, Σx2ix3i grows, D shrinks towards zero, and both partial variances blow up. The data is being asked to separate two things that move together, and it says so by returning enormous standard errors.

Estimating σ2

σ̂2 = Σ ûi2 / (n − 3)

Note the similarity to its two-variable counterpart. The degrees of freedom are now n − 3 because in estimating Σûi2 we must first estimate β̂1, β̂2 and β̂3, which consume three degrees of freedom. The argument is quite general: in the k-variable case the degrees of freedom are n − k, with k counting the intercept.

7 · Properties of the fitted surface

i The regression surface passes through the means Ȳ, X̄2 and X̄3 — which is just β̂1 = Ȳ − β̂2X̄2 − β̂3X̄3 rearranged. It generalises to the k-variable model.
ii The mean of the fitted values equals the mean of the actual values: Ȳ̂ = Ȳ.
iii The residuals have zero mean, Σûi = 0, and are uncorrelated with every regressor and with Ŷi.
Proof that Ȳ̂ = Ȳ, and the deviation form of the model

Sum the SRF over the sample: ΣYi = nβ̂1 + β̂2ΣX2i + β̂3ΣX3i + Σûi, and the first normal equation says Σûi = 0. Dividing by n gives Ȳ = β̂1 + β̂2X̄2 + β̂3X̄3, and the right-hand side is precisely Ȳ̂. So Ȳ̂ = Ȳ.

Subtracting that from the SRF term by term puts the whole model in deviation form, which is the form the derivations use:

yi = β̂2x2i + β̂3x3i + ûi  ·  ŷi = β̂2x2i + β̂3x3i

Note that the intercept has vanished. That is the point of deviation form: it is already absorbed in the means.

8 · Goodness of fit: R2

In the two-variable case, r2 gives the percentage of the total variation in Y explained by the single explanatory variable X. With three variables we want the percentage of the variation in Y explained by X2 and X3 jointly. That quantity is the multiple coefficient of determination, written with a capital R2.

  1. Start from the deviation form, square and sum. The cross terms vanish for the same reason as in Unit 1C — the residuals are uncorrelated with the fitted values:

    Σ yi2 = Σ ŷi2 + Σ ûi2  ⟹  TSS = ESS + RSS

  2. With ŷi = β̂2x2i + β̂3x3i, the explained sum of squares has a convenient closed form:

    ESS = β̂2 Σ x2iyi + β̂3 Σ x3iyi

  3. Dividing through by TSS gives the definition:

    R2 = ESS/TSS = 1 − RSS/TSS = ( β̂2 Σ x2iyi + β̂3 Σ x3iyi ) / Σ yi2

    Every quantity on the right is computed routinely, so R2 costs nothing extra.

Just like r2, R2 lies between 0 and 1. R2 = 1 means 100% of the variation in Y is explained by the fitted regression; R2 = 0 means none of it is. In most cases it lies in between, and the closer to 1 the better the fit.

The problem: R2 never falls

An important property, and a trap R2 is a non-decreasing function of the number of regressors. Unless an added variable is perfectly collinear with those already there, adding it almost invariably increases R2 and never decreases it. An additional X variable will not reduce R2.

The reason is visible in the definition. Σyi2 is Σ(Yi − Ȳ)2, which depends only on Y and is therefore independent of how many regressors are in the model. The RSS, Σûi2, does depend on them — and as variables are added, the residual sum of squares is likely to fall and cannot rise, because the extra regressor can always be given a coefficient of zero if it is of no use. A smaller RSS over a fixed TSS is a larger R2.

So when choosing between two models with the same dependent variable but a different number of independent variables, one must account for the number of variables before comparing their R2. Watch what happens when the regressors added are known to be worthless:

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It plots R2 and adjusted R2 against the number of pure-noise regressors added to a correctly specified model; R2 rises throughout while the adjusted measure falls.

Adjusted R2

R̄2 = 1 − (1 − R2) · (n − 1) / (n − k)

where k is the number of parameters including the intercept. The term adjusted means adjusted for the degrees of freedom associated with the sums of squares entering the model. Each extra regressor lowers n − k, which pushes R̄2 down; the extra explanatory power pushes it up. R̄2 rises only when the second effect beats the first — that is, when the variable earns its place.

Three things about R̄2 i. R̄2 ≤ R2 always, with equality only when k = 1.
ii. Unlike R2, it can be negative — which is a blunt signal that the model is worse than simply predicting Ȳ for every observation.
iii. It is the measure to quote when comparing models with different numbers of regressors. It is not a substitute for asking whether a variable belongs in the model on theoretical grounds, and it is not a hypothesis test. Unit 1F provides the test.

9 · Reporting a multiple regression

The house format extends in the obvious way — one column per term, standard errors beneath, t-statistics below those. Extending the running consumption example with a second regressor (illustrative figures):

Consumption̂i =s.e.t = 68.41(12.30)5.56 + 0.62 Incomei(0.08)7.75 + 0.09 Wealthi(0.03)3.00 n = 30  ·  k = 3  ·  R2 = 0.87  ·  R̄2 = 0.86  ·  σ̂ = 9.4
How the interpretation changes 0.62 — for every additional dollar of income, consumption rises by 62 cents holding wealth constant. That qualifier is now compulsory: without it the number is being described as something it is not.
0.09 — for every additional dollar of wealth, consumption rises by 9 cents holding income constant.
68.41 — predicted consumption when income and wealth are both zero. Often, as here, an extrapolation far outside the data; do not over-read it.
R2 = 0.87 — income and wealth jointly explain 87% of the variation in consumption. Note that this cannot be split into "income's share" and "wealth's share" unless the two regressors happen to be uncorrelated.
R̄2 = 0.86 — the same figure after paying for the third parameter. The gap is small here because n is comfortably larger than k.
Compare the slope with Unit 1C In Unit 1C the same consumption function, estimated on income alone, returned a slope of 0.75. Adding wealth pulls it to 0.62. Neither number is a mistake: they are answers to different questions. The first is the total association between income and consumption, wealth moving along with it; the second is the effect of income with wealth held fixed. Which one you want depends on the question — but only the second can be described as a partial effect.

10 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

Show that when Σx2ix3i = 0, the multiple regression estimator β̂2 reduces exactly to the simple regression estimator of Unit 1C. Explain in words what this tells you about when a simple regression is trustworthy.
Show solution

Setting Σx2ix3i = 0 in the numerator kills the second term, leaving (Σx2iyi)(Σx3i2); the denominator becomes (Σx2i2)(Σx3i2). The Σx3i2 cancels and β̂2 = Σx2iyi/Σx2i2, which is the Unit 1C formula.

In words: when the regressors are uncorrelated in the sample, including X3 or omitting it makes no difference at all to the estimated effect of X2. A simple regression is therefore trustworthy precisely when the omitted variables are uncorrelated with the included one — which is exactly the condition the figure in section 2 dials up and down, and exactly the condition that fails in the school-spending example.

A colleague fits a model with 4 regressors to 12 observations and reports R2 = 0.91. Compute the adjusted R2 and comment. What would happen to each measure if they added three more regressors of random numbers?
Show solution

With 4 regressors plus an intercept, k = 5, n = 12, so n − k = 7 and n − 1 = 11:

R̄2 = 1 − (1 − 0.91)(11/7) = 1 − 0.09 × 1.571 = 0.859

The gap between 0.91 and 0.859 is already substantial, and it is a warning: with only 7 degrees of freedom left, a good deal of that fit is being bought with parameters rather than with explanation.

Adding three columns of random numbers: R2 would rise, or at worst stay the same — it cannot fall, because the RSS cannot rise when a regressor is added. R̄2 would almost certainly fall, because n − k drops from 7 to 4 and the multiplier (n − 1)/(n − k) jumps from 1.571 to 2.75, while random columns add essentially nothing to R2. This is the whole argument for never comparing models on R2 alone.

Using var(β̂2) = σ2Σx3i2/D with D = Σx2i2Σx3i2 − (Σx2ix3i)2, explain what happens to the precision of β̂2 as X2 and X3 become more strongly correlated. What happens in the limit?
Show solution

As the correlation between the regressors rises, the cross-product Σx2ix3i grows in absolute value, so (Σx2ix3i)2 grows and D — the difference — falls. D is the denominator, so var(β̂2) rises: the estimate becomes less precise, its standard error larger, and its t-statistic smaller.

In the limit of perfect correlation, X3 is an exact linear function of X2, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality holds with equality, D = 0, and neither β̂2 nor its variance is defined. That is assumption 8 failing. The intuition is that the data contains no observation in which X2 moves while X3 stays put, so there is simply no evidence about what X2 does on its own. The intermediate, everyday case — high but not perfect correlation, large standard errors, unstable coefficients — is Unit 2B.

A researcher regresses hourly wage on education alone and finds a coefficient of 0.90. Adding a measure of innate ability to the model, the coefficient on education falls to 0.55. Explain what has happened, state which assumption the first regression violated, and say which of the two numbers answers the question "what would an extra year of school do for me?"
Show solution

What happened. Ability affects wages and is positively correlated with education. Omitted from the first regression, it sits in the disturbance, so cov(education, u) ≠ 0 and assumption 3 fails — equivalently, assumption 9, no specification bias. The estimated coefficient on education then picks up both the direct effect of schooling and the effect of the ability that tends to accompany it. Adding ability to the model holds it constant, and the 0.35 difference is the part that was never education's to begin with.

Which number answers the question. The 0.55 — it is the partial effect of education holding ability constant, which is what an individual with fixed ability considering another year of school wants to know. The 0.90 is the average difference in wages between people with more and less education, ability and all, which is a description of the population rather than a statement about a decision.

Two cautions worth stating. This assumes ability has been measured well; a poor proxy only partly closes the gap. And it assumes no further omitted variable correlated with education — which is why Unit 3B exists.

11 · Check yourself

In Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui, the coefficient β2 measures
Correct — a partial coefficient. The "holding X3 constant" clause is part of what the number means, not an optional gloss.
β2 is a partial coefficient: the change in the mean of Y per unit change in X2 with X3 held constant. The total change in Y would also include whatever X3 does when X2 moves.
Adding an extra regressor to a model can never reduce R2, because
Correct — TSS depends only on Y, and RSS can always be held where it was by giving the new variable a coefficient of zero.
TSS = Σ(Yi − Ȳ)² depends only on Y, so it does not move. RSS cannot rise, since OLS could always set the new coefficient to zero and reproduce the old fit. A weakly smaller RSS over a fixed TSS is a weakly larger R².
In the three-variable model, σ̂2 = Σûi2/(n − 3) because
Correct — β̂1, β̂2 and β̂3 consume three degrees of freedom, and in general the divisor is n − k.
No observations are lost. The three normal equations impose three restrictions on the residuals, so only n − 3 of them are free to vary. In the k-variable model the divisor is n − k, with k counting the intercept.
Two regressors are strongly but not perfectly correlated. The most likely symptom is
Correct — D shrinks as the cross-product grows, so both partial variances inflate. The estimates stay unbiased; they just become imprecise.
Correlated regressors do not bias OLS — every assumption still holds. What they do is shrink D = Σx₂²Σx₃² − (Σx₂x₃)², which is the denominator of both variances, so the standard errors inflate and t-statistics collapse. That is Unit 2B.
