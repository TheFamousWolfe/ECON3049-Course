Qualitative Variables

Dummies for categories, interactions for slopes, and the trap of the dummy variable.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • construct a dummy variable for a qualitative regressor, and say what its coefficient measures;
  • identify the base category, and rewrite a set of dummy coefficients under a different base;
  • state the m − 1 rule, and explain the dummy variable trap as perfect multicollinearity;
  • interpret a dummy coefficient alongside quantitative regressors, as a shift in the intercept;
  • interpret a dummy coefficient when the dependent variable is logged, approximately and exactly;
  • build an interaction between a dummy and a quantitative variable, and test whether the slopes differ;
  • test whether two groups share the same regression, and relate that test to the Chow test.
Where this sits Unit 2 took the classical assumptions apart one at a time. Unit 3 turns to problems of a different kind, and this one is the friendliest of them: nothing here is a violation to be detected and repaired. Dummy variables are a modelling device, and every result about OLS carries over untouched. Two earlier units do most of the work. Unit 2B's perfect multicollinearity is the trap in section 3, and it is the one place in the course where you can create assumption 7's failure by accident in a single keystroke. Unit 2A Part 2's interaction terms and semi-log forms return in sections 6 and 7, this time with a dummy on one side of the product.

1 · Qualitative information

Everything so far has related quantitative variables to one another — income to consumption, price to quantity, education to wages. But many variables of interest are not quantitative at all. Sex, marital status, region, race, occupation, whether a household owns its home, whether a country is an oil exporter: these are qualitative, or categorical, variables. They record which category an observation belongs to, and nothing about them is naturally a number.

Our focus is the case that arises most often: a qualitative independent variable with a quantitative dependent variable. The question is simply how to get such a variable into a regression, given that OLS can only multiply a coefficient by a number.

The marital wage premium

An observation in the wage determination literature is that married men earn more than single men. In the sample the lecture works with, the average wage is $5.17 an hour for single men and $7.98 for married men — a gap of $2.81, which is more than half of what the single men earn. A researcher who wants to say anything careful about that gap needs to put marital status into an equation.

The device Create a dummy variable — also called a binary, indicator or dichotomous variable — that takes the value 1 when the observation is in a category and 0 when it is not:

marriedi = 1 if man i is married, 0 if he is single

There is nothing arbitrary about 1 and 0 that matters for the algebra, but the choice does fix the interpretation of everything that follows, which is the subject of section 2.

With that variable in hand the regression is an ordinary one:

wagei = β1 + β2 marriedi + ui

and it is estimated by ordinary least squares in exactly the way Unit 1B set out. Nothing about the estimator changes. A dummy variable is a regressor like any other; it simply happens to take only two values.

2 · What the coefficients mean

Estimating that equation on the wage sample gives:

wagêi =p = 5.1680.000 + 2.815 marriedi0.000 the slides report p-values in place of standard errors

Read the fitted equation at each of the two values the dummy can take, which is the whole of the interpretation:

single men: marriedi = 0  ⟹  wagê = 5.168

married men: marriedi = 1  ⟹  wagê = 5.168 + 2.815 = 7.983

Those are the two averages the section opened with, recovered to the cent. That is not a coincidence, and it is worth seeing why.

A regression on a single dummy returns the group means With one dummy and a constant, the fitted value has only two values available to it, so least squares must choose the pair that minimises the sum of squared deviations within each group. For any group of numbers that minimiser is the group's own mean. Hence, exactly,

β̂1 = Ȳ0  ·  β̂2 = Ȳ1 − Ȳ0

where Ȳ0 is the mean of the category coded 0 and Ȳ1 the mean of the category coded 1. The intercept is the base group's mean; the dummy coefficient is the difference between the two means. Everything else in this unit is a variation on that sentence.

Because the coefficient is a difference in means, its t-test is a test of whether the two group means differ — the difference-of-means test met in a statistics course, obtained here as an ordinary regression output. With a p-value of 0.000 the difference is comfortably significant.

The base category

The category coded 0 is the base category — also called the reference, benchmark or omitted category. It does not appear in the equation, and everything the equation reports is measured relative to it. Nothing forces the choice, and it is instructive to make the other one. Define singlei = 1 for single men and 0 for married men, and estimate again:

wagêi =p = 7.9830.000 − 2.815 singlei0.000 the same regression, with married men as the base category

The intercept is now the married men's mean and the dummy coefficient has changed sign. Not one number in the two tables is the same, and yet nothing at all has changed: the two fitted values are 7.983 and 5.168 either way, the residuals are identical, the R2 is identical, and the p-value on the difference is identical. The choice of base category changes how the answer is written down, not what the answer is. Choose the base that makes the comparisons you care about easiest to read, and always say in the text which category it is — a table of dummy coefficients without its base category is uninterpretable.

3 · The dummy variable trap

Notice what neither regression did: include both dummies. There is an obvious temptation to write

wagei = β1 + β2 marriedi + β3 singlei + ui

so that each category gets its own coefficient. This cannot be estimated, and the reason is Unit 2B's. Write the intercept as a coefficient on a variable X1 that is a column of ones — which is what an intercept is — and set out the three columns for a sample of six men, two single and four married:

X1 = (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1)′  ·  married = (1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0)′  ·  single = (0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1)′

Every man is either married or single, so marriedi + singlei = 1 = X1i for every observation without exception. That is an exact linear relationship between the regressors: perfect multicollinearity, assumption 7 failing, and the normal equations have no unique solution. Software will refuse to estimate the model, or silently drop one of the columns. This is the dummy variable trap.

The rule If a qualitative variable has m categories, include at most m − 1 dummy variables for it alongside a constant.
Alternatively, include all m dummies and suppress the constant. Each coefficient is then a group mean outright rather than a difference from a base. It is a valid regression, but it is not the intuitive one: with no intercept, the reported R2 cannot be compared with anything else, and Unit 2A Part 2's warning about a suppressed constant applies in full. The m − 1 form is the one to use unless you have a specific reason not to.

The trap is worth recognising in the forms it takes outside a textbook. A set of expenditure shares that sum to one, entered alongside a constant, is the same failure. So are quarterly dummies for all four quarters plus an intercept, and a full set of regional dummies plus an intercept. In every case the offence is identical: a set of columns that adds up to the column of ones.

4 · More than two categories

Suppose the qualitative variable has three categories rather than two — occupation, recorded as professional, clerical or service. The rule says two dummies alongside a constant, and there are three ways to choose them. Take clerical and service:

clericali = 1 if the occupation is clerical, 0 otherwise  ·  servicei = 1 if service, 0 otherwise

The category left out — professional — is the base, and the fitted equation is:

wagêi =p = 6.6460.000 − 1.909 clericali0.000 − 3.057 servicei0.000 base category: professional

Reading it category by category, as in section 2: professionals average 6.646, clerical workers 6.646 − 1.909 = 4.737, and service workers 6.646 − 3.057 = 3.589. Each coefficient is a comparison with the base, and no coefficient in this table compares clerical with service directly — although their difference, 1.148, does.

Choose clerical as the base instead and the same three means are written differently. The intercept becomes the clerical mean, 4.737; the professional dummy carries 6.646 − 4.737 = 1.909; and the service dummy carries 3.589 − 4.737 = −1.148:

wagêi =p = 4.7370.000 + 1.909 professionali0.000 − 1.148 servicei— base category: clerical — the same regression, re-based

The p-values do not carry over, and that is the one thing re-basing genuinely changes. Each t-test is a test against the base, so switching base switches the comparisons being tested. The service coefficient in the first table asks whether service workers differ from professionals; in the second it asks whether they differ from clerical workers. Those are different questions and can perfectly well have different answers.

Reconciling two tables Two dummy tables from the same data must agree about the fitted group means, because they are the same regression written with a different base. Recovering the means from one table and rebuilding the other from them is a two-line piece of arithmetic, and it is the quickest check that you have read a table correctly. If a pair of tables you are given cannot be reconciled that way, they are not the same regression — different samples, or different controls, or a slip in transcription.

A name for these models Gujarati calls a regression whose regressors are all dummies an ANOVA model, because it reproduces the analysis of variance, and one that mixes dummies with quantitative regressors an ANCOVA model — analysis of covariance. Sections 1 to 4 are ANOVA; everything from section 5 on is ANCOVA. The names are worth knowing when reading the chapter, but nothing in the estimation depends on them.

5 · Dummies alongside quantitative regressors

The marital premium of $2.81 compares married and single men without controlling for anything else. Married men may differ from single men in ways that affect wages on their own account, and the obvious candidate is education. Is the gap still there once education is held constant?

wagei = β1 + β2 marriedi + β3 Educi + ui

Take the conditional expectation for each group in turn. For single men marriedi = 0, and for married men it is 1, so:

E(wage | Educ, single) = β1 + β3 Educ

E(wage | Educ, married) = (β1 + β2) + β3 Educ

Two parallel lines These are two straight lines in education with different intercepts and the same slope. β1 is the intercept for the base category, β1 + β2 the intercept for the other, and the vertical distance between them is β2 at every level of education. For this reason β2 is called the differential intercept coefficient.
A dummy entered on its own can do nothing else. It shifts the line up or down; it cannot tilt it. Tilting is section 7's job.

Estimated on the wage data, the equation is:

wagêi =p = 2.0140.040 + 1.260 marriedi0.000 + 3.301 Educi0.000 the premium, holding education constant

The premium survives, but it is much smaller: $1.26 rather than $2.81. Slightly more than half of the raw gap was education rather than marriage — married men in this sample are better educated, and the simple comparison in section 2 credited marriage with what education was doing. That is Unit 2A Part 1's omitted variable bias, appearing here as the reason for running the regression at all. Whether the $1.26 that remains is discrimination, or productivity, or something else the equation still omits, is not a question the regression can answer.

Mind the units A coefficient is always per unit of whatever the regressor measures, and a dummy is the only regressor whose units are never in doubt. Before quoting a slope, check it against the mean of the dependent variable: $3.30 an hour per unit of education, against an average wage under $8, says that the education variable here cannot be measured in single years — the semi-log equation in section 6 puts a year of education at about 9%, which on a $7 wage is about 65 cents. Nothing is wrong with either estimate; they are answers to differently scaled questions. In an examination, state the units you are assuming before you interpret a magnitude.

6 · A logged dependent variable

Wage equations are usually estimated in logs, for the reasons Unit 2A Part 2 gave: the distribution of wages is skewed, the semi-log form makes the coefficients percentages, and it handles Unit 2C's heteroscedasticity into the bargain. Adding experience as well:

ln(wagei) = β1 + β2 marriedi + β3 Educi + β4 Experi + ui

ln(wage)̂i =p = 0.2440.080 + 0.287 marriedi0.000 + 0.093 Educi0.000 + 0.011 Experi0.000 a log-lin model: the coefficients are semi-elasticities

Note first what is not logged. A dummy variable is never logged, for the plain reason that ln(0) is undefined — half the sample would vanish. The transformation is applied to the dependent variable and to strictly positive quantitative regressors, and to nothing else.

For the quantitative regressors the interpretation is Unit 2A Part 2's: multiply by 100 and read a percentage, so a year of education is worth about 9.3% and a year of experience about 1.1%. For the dummy the same rule gives 28.7% — married men earn about 28.7% more, holding education and experience constant — but here the approximation deserves a second look.

The exact percentage 100β̂2 approximates the percentage difference, and the approximation is good only for small β̂. A dummy switches from 0 to 1, which is a whole unit rather than a marginal change, so its coefficient is often large enough for the gap to matter. The exact percentage difference between the two categories is

100 · [ exp(β̂2) − 1 ] %

which here is 100(e0.287 − 1) = 33.2%, against the approximate 28.7%. A four-and-a-half point difference on a headline number is worth the extra keystroke, and the approximation always understates a positive effect.

7 · Interactions: letting the slopes differ

Every model so far has assumed that the partial effect of education is the same for married and single men. That is what a common slope means, and it is an assumption, not a finding. It is perfectly possible that education pays differently for the two groups. To find out, interact the dummy with the variable whose effect is in question:

ln(wagei) = β1 + β2 marriedi + β3 Educi + β4 Experi + β5 ( marriedi · Educi ) + ui

The interaction term is the product of the two variables, computed and entered as an ordinary regressor — exactly the construction of Unit 2A Part 2, with a dummy in place of a second quantitative variable. Differentiate to see what it buys:

∂ln(wage) / ∂Educ = β3 + β5 married

single men: ∂ln(wage) / ∂Educ = β3  ·  married men: ∂ln(wage) / ∂Educ = β3 + β5

Differential intercept, differential slope β2 shifts the intercept and β5 tilts the line, so β5 is the differential slope coefficient. With both in the equation the two groups have their own intercept and their own slope, which is to say two entirely separate lines.
And β2 is no longer "the marital premium". It is the premium at Educ = 0, which in a wage sample is an extrapolation beyond anyone in the data. The premium at an education level of interest is β2 + β5·Educ, and that is the quantity to quote.

Estimating the interacted equation:

ln(wage)̂i =p = 0.4030.060 + 0.047 marriedi0.000 + 0.080 Educi0.000 + 0.011 Experi0.000 + 0.019 (married·Educ)i0.328 education pays 8.0% a year to single men and 9.9% to married men

The test is a t-test on a single coefficient, exactly as in Unit 1F:

H0: β5 = 0 (no difference in the partial effect of education)

H1: β5 ≠ 0 (the partial effect of education differs)

The p-value is 0.328, far above any conventional significance level, so we cannot reject the null. There is no evidence in this sample that education pays differently for married and single men, and the parallel-lines model of section 6 is the one to report.

Before leaving the table, reconcile it with section 6, because at first reading the two look contradictory. The marital coefficient has collapsed from 0.287 to 0.047 — but 0.047 is the premium at zero education, while 0.287 was the premium at every education level. Evaluate the interacted model at a typical schooling of 12 or 13 years:

0.047 + 0.019 × 12 = 0.275  ·  0.047 + 0.019 × 13 = 0.294

which brackets the 0.287 of the simpler equation. The two models agree about the men in the sample and disagree only about a hypothetical man with no education at all. This is the standard reason a coefficient appears to change dramatically when an interaction is added, and reading it as "the premium fell" is the standard mistake.

8 · Testing whether two groups share a regression

The last question is the general one. Rather than asking whether a single slope differs, ask whether married and single men have the same wage equation at all. There are two ways to go about it.

i Run two separate regressions, one on each group, and compare them. This is the Chow test of Unit 2A Part 2, where the split was by time period rather than by category, and the arithmetic is identical.
ii Run one equation containing a constant, the dummy, every quantitative regressor, and the dummy interacted with every one of them. The restrictions can then be tested inside a single model.

Take the second route. The pooled equation, which imposes one wage function on everybody, is

ln(wagei) = α1 + α2 Educi + α3 Experi + ui

and the fully interacted equation, which allows married and single men everything, is

ln(wagei) = β1 + β2 marriedi + β3 Educi + β4 Experi + β5 ( married · Educ )i + β6 ( married · Exper )i + ei

If the two groups share a wage function then the dummy and both of its interactions are superfluous:

H0: β2 = β5 = β6 = 0

which is three restrictions and therefore a standard F-test, in the restricted-versus-unrestricted form of Unit 1F:

F = [ (RSSR − RSSUR) / m ] / [ RSSUR / (n − k) ]   ~   F(3, n − 6)

with m = 3 restrictions and k = 6 parameters in the unrestricted model. Reject and the groups differ somewhere; fail to reject and one wage function will do for both.

Why this is the flexible version The single-equation form lets you test which part of the equation differs, simply by choosing the restriction:
β2 = 0 alone — do the intercepts differ? (a t-test)
β5 = β6 = 0 — do any slopes differ, the intercepts being free? (an F-test on two restrictions)
β5 = 0 alone — does the return to education differ? (section 7)
β2 = β5 = β6 = 0 — is it the same equation throughout?
A pair of separate regressions answers only the last question, and answers it with more arithmetic.

One identity ties the two routes together and is worth carrying into an examination. The fully interacted regression is the two separate regressions, run at once: its fitted line for each group is precisely the line that group's own subsample produces, and therefore

RSSUR = RSS1 + RSS2

the residual sum of squares of the interacted model equals the sum of the two subsample residual sums of squares. Substituting that into the F formula gives the Chow statistic exactly. The figure in section 5 shows the identity holding at every setting of its slider.

The two routes are not quite interchangeable, though. The interacted equation estimates a single error variance from the pooled residuals, so it assumes the disturbance variance is the same in both groups — the assumption the Chow test makes, and a form of Unit 2C's homoscedasticity. Two separate regressions estimate a separate variance for each group and are therefore the safer choice when the groups plainly differ in dispersion, at the price of losing the convenient single-equation tests.

9 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

A regression of hourly wages on a married dummy gives wagê = 5.168 + 2.815 married. (a) State the two fitted group means. (b) Write down the regression that would result from using a single dummy instead. (c) Write down the version with both dummies and no intercept. (d) A colleague proposes including both dummies and an intercept. Explain precisely what goes wrong.
Show solution

(a) The group means. Set the dummy to each of its values: single men 5.168, married men 5.168 + 2.815 = 7.983. The intercept is the base group's mean and the dummy coefficient is the difference between the means.

(b) With a single dummy. The base becomes married men, so the intercept is their mean and the coefficient is the difference the other way round:

wagê = 7.983 − 2.815 single

The fitted values, residuals and R2 are unchanged; only the parameterisation differs.

(c) With no intercept. Each coefficient becomes a group mean outright:

wagê = 7.983 married + 5.168 single

Valid, and occasionally convenient, but the reported R2 is not comparable with that of any model containing a constant, and no coefficient is the difference we actually want to test.

(d) Both dummies and an intercept. marriedi + singlei = 1 for every observation, which is exactly the column of ones the intercept multiplies. The regressors are perfectly collinear, X′X is singular, and no unique least squares solution exists — the dummy variable trap. With m categories, use at most m − 1 dummies alongside a constant.

Wages are regressed on occupation dummies with professional as the base category, giving wagê = 6.646 − 1.909 clerical − 3.057 service. (a) Report the three fitted group means. (b) Rewrite the equation with service as the base. (c) Rewrite it with no intercept. (d) The clerical coefficient in the first equation has a p-value of 0.000. Does it follow that clerical and service wages differ significantly?
Show solution

(a) The means. Professional 6.646; clerical 6.646 − 1.909 = 4.737; service 6.646 − 3.057 = 3.589.

(b) Service as the base. The intercept becomes 3.589, and each remaining coefficient is that group's mean minus 3.589:

wagê = 3.589 + 3.057 professional + 1.148 clerical

(c) No intercept. Every coefficient is a group mean:

wagê = 6.646 professional + 4.737 clerical + 3.589 service

All three equations produce identical fitted values for every observation. They are one regression in three sets of clothes.

(d) No, it does not follow. The p-value on the clerical dummy tests clerical against the base, which here is professional. The clerical–service comparison is the difference between two estimated coefficients, and its standard error involves their covariance, so it cannot be read off either coefficient's own test. The practical route is to re-run the regression with service (or clerical) as the base, which puts the comparison you want on a single coefficient and gives you its test directly. This is the one substantive reason to prefer one base category over another.

A log wage equation returns a coefficient of 0.287 on a married dummy. (a) Give the approximate and the exact percentage premium. (b) Explain why the two differ, and in which direction the approximation errs. (c) Why is the married dummy not logged along with the wage? (d) At what size of coefficient would you insist on the exact figure?
Show solution

(a) The two figures. Approximately 100 × 0.287 = 28.7%. Exactly:

100 [ exp(0.287) − 1 ] = 100 (1.3324 − 1) = 33.2%

(b) Why they differ. The coefficient in a log-lin model is ∂ln(Y)/∂X, a proportional change per marginal unit of X, and 100β̂ is the first-order approximation to it. A dummy does not move marginally: it moves a whole unit, from 0 to 1, so the linearisation is evaluated far from the point it is accurate at. Since exp(β) − 1 > β for every β > 0, the approximation understates a positive effect, and the shortfall grows with the coefficient.

(c) Why the dummy is not logged. ln(0) is undefined, so logging the dummy would destroy every observation in the base category. Logs are applied to the dependent variable and to strictly positive quantitative regressors only.

(d) When to insist. At 0.05 the two agree to a tenth of a point and nothing turns on the choice; at 0.287 the gap is 4.5 points; at 0.5 it is 50% against 64.9%, which no reader would call the same number. A reasonable working rule is to quote the exact figure whenever |β̂| exceeds about 0.1, and always when the number is going into a headline.

An interacted log wage equation gives ln(wage)̂ = 0.403 + 0.047 married + 0.080 Educ + 0.011 Exper + 0.019 (married·Educ), with a p-value of 0.328 on the interaction. (a) Give the return to education for each group. (b) Give the marital premium at 12 and at 16 years of education. (c) Test whether the returns to education differ. (d) A student concludes that "controlling for the interaction, the marital premium falls from 28.7% to 4.7%". Correct them.
Show solution

(a) The returns to education. ∂ln(wage)/∂Educ = β3 + β5married, so single men 0.080 and married men 0.080 + 0.019 = 0.099 — about 8.0% and 9.9% a year, or 8.3% and 10.4% if the exact conversion is used.

(b) The premium at two education levels. The premium is β2 + β5Educ:

at 12 years: 0.047 + 0.019(12) = 0.275  →  100(e0.275 − 1) = 31.7%

at 16 years: 0.047 + 0.019(16) = 0.351  →  100(e0.351 − 1) = 42.1%

(c) The test. H0: β5 = 0 against H1: β5 ≠ 0, a t-test on one coefficient. The p-value of 0.328 exceeds 0.05 — indeed it exceeds 0.10 — so we do not reject the null. There is no evidence that the return to education differs between the groups, and the parallel-lines model is the one to report. Note that the point estimate of 0.019 is not small in economic terms; it is simply too imprecisely estimated to distinguish from zero, which is a statement about the sample rather than about the world.

(d) The correction. The 0.047 is not the marital premium. Once the interaction is in the model, the coefficient on the dummy is the premium at Educ = 0, and nobody in the sample has no education at all, so it is an extrapolation and not a summary of anything. The premium the data speak to is 0.047 + 0.019·Educ, which at 12 to 13 years is 0.275 to 0.294 — either side of the 0.287 the equation without the interaction reported. The two models agree; only the parameterisation changed.

You wish to test whether married and single men have the same wage equation in Educ and Exper. (a) Write down the restricted and unrestricted models and the null hypothesis. (b) Give the test statistic and its distribution for n = 526. (c) Show how the same equation would test a difference in intercepts only. (d) What does this procedure assume that two separate regressions do not?
Show solution

(a) The two models. Restricted — one equation for everybody:

ln(wage) = α1 + α2Educ + α3Exper + u

Unrestricted — the dummy and both interactions:

ln(wage) = β1 + β2married + β3Educ + β4Exper + β5(married·Educ) + β6(married·Exper) + e

H0: β2 = β5 = β6 = 0

(b) The statistic. Three restrictions, six parameters in the unrestricted model:

F = [ (RSSR − RSSUR) / 3 ] / [ RSSUR / (526 − 6) ] ~ F(3, 520)

Reject for large F. Because the unrestricted model fits each group with its own line, RSSUR = RSSmarried + RSSsingle, and substituting that gives the Chow statistic of Unit 2A Part 2 — the same test, arrived at from the other direction.

(c) Intercepts only. Impose β5 = β6 = 0 from the start — that is, drop the interactions — and test β2 = 0 with an ordinary t-test. That is precisely the model of section 5: two parallel lines, tested for a vertical gap. The single-equation approach lets you choose the restriction, which is why it is more flexible than running the two regressions separately.

(d) The assumption. A single equation estimates one error variance from the pooled residuals, so it assumes the disturbances have the same variance in both groups. Two separate regressions estimate σ2 separately for each. If the groups differ in dispersion — a real possibility in wage data — the pooled standard errors are wrong in the way Unit 2C described, and the F-test with them. Check the groups' residual variances before relying on the single-equation test.
