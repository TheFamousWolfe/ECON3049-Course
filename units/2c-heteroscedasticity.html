Heteroscedasticity

Non-constant error variance: detecting it, and the four ways to live with it.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • state the assumption that heteroscedasticity violates, and write the variance of β̂2 when it fails;
  • distinguish pure from impure heteroscedasticity, and say why the remedies differ;
  • list the consequences for OLS, and say which properties survive;
  • read a plot of squared residuals against fitted values;
  • carry out the Breusch–Pagan and White tests, and state what each can and cannot detect;
  • count the degrees of freedom a White test spends, and say why that matters;
  • choose among logging, robust standard errors, weighted least squares and FGLS.
Where this sits Unit 2B was a problem with the data: the sample could not separate two effects, though every assumption held. This unit is a genuine assumption failure. Assumption 5 breaks, and with it goes the Gauss–Markov theorem and every standard error the regression prints. The coefficients themselves are the one thing that survives untouched.
On the spelling The word is written with either a c or a k in the middle, and both are standard. The lecture slides use the k; the course outline and this site use the c throughout. Nothing turns on it, but be prepared to meet both in the textbooks — Gujarati and Porter use the c, Wooldridge the k.

1 · The assumption, and what breaks

One of the classical assumptions is homoscedasticity: each disturbance, conditional on the chosen values of the explanatory variables, has the same variance.

var(ui) = σ2 = a constant   (i = 1, 2, …, N)

In reality this assumption is often violated. When it fails, the variance of the error term is not constant but depends on the observation:

var(ui) = σi2   (i = 1, 2, …, N)

The subscript on σ2 is the entire content of the problem. It is more prevalent in models using cross-sectional data, though it can occur in time series models as well, and it often turns up in data sets where there is a large disparity between the largest and smallest observed values of the dependent variable. A regression across countries, or across firms, or across households will have this feature almost by construction: the units are not of comparable size, so there is no reason to expect the unexplained part of Y to be of comparable size either.

2 · Pure and impure heteroscedasticity

Heteroscedasticity divides into two versions, and the distinction determines the remedy.

Pure Caused by the error term of a correctly specified equation. The model is right; the disturbance simply does not have constant variance. This is assumption 5 failing on its own.
Impure Caused by specification error — a missing variable, or the wrong functional form. Here the non-constant variance is a symptom rather than the disease: what has actually happened is a violation of E(u | X) = 0, and the pattern in the residuals is the omitted piece of the model showing through.
Why the distinction earns its place With impure heteroscedasticity the correct remedy is to find the omitted variable and include it, or change the functional form. Reaching instead for robust standard errors or weighted least squares would leave a biased model with tidier-looking standard errors, which is worse than the problem you started with — you would have corrected the symptom and preserved the disease. This is why every list of remedies in section 9 begins by re-examining the specification.

3 · Why pure heteroscedasticity arises

1 Error-learning models. As people learn, their errors of behaviour become smaller over time, or the number of errors becomes more consistent.
2 Discretionary income. As incomes grow, people have more scope for choice about the disposition of their income — so consumption at high incomes is more variable around its mean than consumption at low incomes, where most of it is committed.
3 Improving data collection. As data-collecting techniques improve, σi2 is likely to decrease.
4 Outliers. An outlying observation is one much different, either very small or very large, in relation to the rest of the sample. A few of them are enough to make the variance look as though it depends on where in the sample you are.
5 Skewness in a regressor. Skewness in the distribution of one or more regressors included in the model — income, wealth and education are the standard examples, and all three are common right-hand-side variables.

4 · What it does to OLS

Suppose we let E(ui2) = σi2 but retain every other assumption of the classical model. Revert to the two-variable model:

Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui

Applying the usual formula, the OLS estimator of β2 is

β̂2 = Σxiyi / Σxi2 = [ nΣXiYi − ΣXiΣYi ] / [ nΣXi2 − (ΣXi)2 ]

Nothing in that expression mentions σ2. The OLS estimators are the same under pure heteroscedasticity — the arithmetic of fitting the line does not know or care whether the variance is constant. What changes is the variance of that estimator:

var(β̂2) = Σxi2σi2 / ( Σxi2 )2

which is obviously different from the usual formula obtained under homoscedasticity:

var(β̂2) = σ2 / Σxi2

The one contains the other Set every σi2 equal to a common σ2 and it comes out of the sum: Σxi2σ2 / (Σxi2)2 = σ2Σxi2 / (Σxi2)2 = σ2 / Σxi2. The familiar formula is the special case, which is worth seeing: the software that printed your standard error assumed the special case whether or not it holds.

The three consequences

i No bias and no inconsistency. The coefficient estimates are unaffected. Note, though, that a lack of bias does not guarantee accuracy — an unbiased estimate drawn from a wide distribution can still be a long way from the truth.
ii OLS becomes inefficient. It no longer has the minimum variance of all the linear unbiased estimators, because the Gauss–Markov theorem needs assumption 5 and no longer has it. Some other linear unbiased estimator — weighted least squares, in section 9 — can now beat it.
iii The estimated standard errors are biased. The regression reports √(σ̂2/Σxi2) when the truth is the other formula, so hypothesis tests and confidence intervals are unreliable — t-ratios, F-tests and interval estimates all inherit the error.
Which way is the bias? There is no general answer, and an examination answer that asserts one is wrong. The conventional formula understates the truth when σi2 is positively associated with xi2 — that is, when the noisiest observations are also the ones furthest from X̄, which is the common case and the more dangerous one, since it makes t-ratios too large and tests reject too often. Where the association runs the other way the conventional standard error is too conservative instead.

5 · The forms it takes

Heteroscedasticity can take many complex forms, and the reasons for it vary. Most often the variance of the error term is related to one or more of the independent variables. For a typical regression equation

Y = β1 + β2X2 + ⋯ + βkXk + u

we can write the conditional variance as

var(u | X) = σi2 = σ2h(Xi) = σ2Zi

where Zi = h(Xi) is some function of the explanatory variables that determines the heteroscedasticity. Two conditions and one warning attach to this.

i h(X) must be positive, since a variance always is. This constraint reappears in section 9 as the reason FGLS works with logarithms.
ii The function may be linear or non-linear. We could easily have σi2 = σ2Xi2, which is the case Zi = Xi2. Which of these two possibilities holds is exactly what separates the two tests in sections 7 and 8.
iii This does not imply E(u | X) ≠ 0. Writing the variance as a function of X says nothing about the mean of the disturbance. Pure heteroscedasticity leaves assumptions 3 and 4 entirely intact, which is precisely why it costs no bias.

6 · Detecting it: the informal methods

Before reaching for a formal test, three things are worth doing, and the first two cost nothing.

  1. Look at the dependent variable. If its maximum value is many, many times larger than its minimum, beware of heteroscedasticity.
  2. Look for specification errors. Are there any obvious ones? Any likely omitted variables? Have you specified a linear model where a double-log model would be more appropriate? This is the impure case of section 2, and it is much the cheapest to fix.
  3. Eyeball the squared residuals. Estimate the regression, collect the residuals and square them: ûi2 = (Y − Ŷ)2. These are not the same as ui2, which we never observe, but they serve as a proxy. Create a scatter plot with ûi2 on the vertical axis and Ŷ on the horizontal. No pattern suggests homoscedasticity; a pattern suggests the opposite. One may also plot the squared residuals against the various independent variables.

7 · The Breusch–Pagan test

After the preliminary investigation it is a good idea to try a formal test. There are many; two are in common use, and the first is designed to detect any linear form of heteroscedasticity, var(u | X) = σ2X. It investigates whether the squared residuals can be explained by the independent variables.

Hypotheses and small print H0: the error variances are all equal — homoscedasticity.
H1: the error variances are a multiplicative function of one or more variables.
It is usually carried out as an LM test against χ2, though some programs also present an F-test version, and it assumes normality of the errors.
  1. Obtain the residuals from the estimated equation, ûi = Yi − Ŷi.
  2. Square them and use them as the dependent variable in an auxiliary equation:

    ûi2 = α1 + α2X2 + α3X3 + ⋯ + αkXk + ε

  3. Save the R2 from that auxiliary regression and test H0: α2 = α3 = ⋯ = αk = 0 against the alternative that at least one α ≠ 0, using

    LM = nR2  ~  χ2(d)

    where n is the sample size and d is the number of independent variables in the auxiliary regression. It is a right-tail test.

Worked example — CO2 emissions and national income

A regression of carbon dioxide emissions on gross national income across 182 countries:

CO2 = 12.28 + 0.44 GNI  ·  n = 182

Following the three steps, the auxiliary regression of the squared residuals on GNI is

û2 = −11,312.17 + 676.61 GNI  ·  R2 = 0.145

so that

LM = nR2 = 182 × 0.145 = 26.39

The 5% critical value of χ2 with one degree of freedom — one independent variable in the auxiliary regression — is 3.84. Since 26.39 far exceeds it, we reject the null hypothesis: there is evidence of heteroscedasticity. Given what the variables are, this should surprise nobody. The gap between the largest and smallest national incomes in a 182-country sample is enormous, which is the very first informal warning sign in section 6.

8 · The White test

The default Breusch–Pagan test investigates linear forms. But as section 5 noted, in the case of multiplicative heteroscedasticity — var(u | X) = σi2 = σ2h(X) — the function h(X) may be non-linear, and a linear auxiliary regression will look straight past it. The White test investigates whether the squared residuals can be explained by the equation's independent variables, their squares, and their cross-products. It is again an LM test, with H0 homoscedasticity against H1 the negation.

  1. Obtain the residuals of the estimated equation, ûi = Yi − Ŷi.
  2. Use the squared residuals as the dependent variable in an auxiliary equation, with each X from the original equation, the square of each X, and the product of each X with every other X as explanatory variables. For a model with two independent variables:

    ûi2 = α1 + α2X2 + α3X3 + α4X2X3 + α5X22 + α6X32 + ε

  3. Collect the R2 and use it to test the overall significance of that equation — H0: α2 = α3 = α4 = α5 = α6 = 0 — with the same χ2 test on nR2.

The same example, tested by White

With only one independent variable there are no cross-terms, so the auxiliary regression adds just the square:

û2 = −289,715.7 + 2,142.486 GNI − 0.124 GNI2  ·  R2 = 0.279

LM = nR2 = 182 × 0.279 = 50.778

The 5% critical value with two degrees of freedom is 5.99, so again we reject: there is evidence of heteroscedasticity. Notice that the White statistic is roughly twice the Breusch–Pagan one on the same data. The squared term has picked up curvature in the variance that the linear auxiliary regression could not represent.

The weakness: White uses up a lot of degrees of freedom As the number of explanatory variables in the original regression rises, the number of right-hand-side variables in the White auxiliary regression goes up much faster — k linear terms, k squares and k(k − 1)/2 cross-products.

The special case that conserves degrees of freedom

A special case of the White test, used to economise, changes step 2. Instead of every variable, its square and its cross-products, regress the squared residuals on the fitted values and their square:

ûi2 = α1 + α2Ŷ + α3Ŷ2 + ε

The test of joint significance is now simply H0: α2 = α3 = 0, which is two degrees of freedom no matter how many regressors the original equation had. The trick is the same one the RESET test of Unit 2A used: Ŷ is a linear combination of all the regressors, so its square carries their squares and cross-products in compressed form.

9 · Remedies

First — re-examine the specification

If heteroscedasticity is found, the first thing to do is examine the equation carefully for specification errors. Are there any omitted variables? Should one or more of the independent variables be non-linear? Is there non-linearity in the parameters? Only if there are no obvious specification errors is the heteroscedasticity probably pure in nature, and only then should one of the following remedies be considered.

Remedy 1 — logging the data

In many cases the problem disappears after logging the data, though it does not always work. It is most useful in instances of outliers, since taking logarithms compresses the upper tail of a distribution far more than the lower, and a variable whose largest value is a thousand times its smallest becomes one whose range is a factor of seven.

The limitation is the one from Unit 2A Part 2: you cannot log every variable. Variables containing zeros or negative numbers have no logarithm, and some variables — a rate already in percent, a variable measured in years — make no intuitive sense logged.

Return to the emissions example and suppose we log both variables:

ln(CO2) = −1.10 + 1.01 ln(GNI)  ·  n = 182

The Breusch–Pagan auxiliary regression is now

û2 = 0.672 − 7.94 × 10−5 ln(GNI)  ·  R2 = 0.000

giving LM = 182 × 0.000 ≈ 0, far below the critical 3.84, so we cannot reject the null: there is no evidence of heteroscedasticity. The White test agrees. Its auxiliary regression

û2 = 0.672 + 0.091 [ln(GNI)]2 − 0.013 ln(GNI)  ·  R2 = 0.004

gives LM = 182 × 0.004 = 0.728 against a critical value of 5.99, so again we cannot reject. One transformation has taken a decisively heteroscedastic equation and made it well behaved, and it has done so without discarding a single observation.

Interactive figure — requires JavaScript. It shows a multiplicative relationship in levels, where the scatter fans out and Breusch–Pagan rejects decisively, and the same observations in logarithms, where the spread is even and the test finds nothing.

Notice what has just happened If logging cures it, the heteroscedasticity was impure — the error variance was never really non-constant, the model was written in the wrong units. A multiplicative relationship forced into a linear equation produces residuals that grow with the level of Y, and that is a functional form error wearing a disguise. Which is the strongest argument for checking the specification first: the cheapest remedy is the one that also fixes the model.

Remedy 2 — heteroscedasticity-corrected standard errors

Weighted least squares and FGLS can be difficult to implement, and one cannot always log the data. The most widely used alternative is to leave the estimates alone and repair only what is broken. Heteroscedasticity-corrected (HC) standard errors, also called heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors, are calculated specifically to avoid the consequences set out in section 4. They are more accurate than uncorrected standard errors, they can be used in t-tests and other hypothesis tests in the ordinary way, and most software packages will produce them on request under one name or another — Huber, White, robust, HC.

What robust standard errors do and do not do They change the standard errors only. The coefficient estimates are identical before and after, because OLS was never biased and there is nothing about the point estimates to correct. Nor do they make OLS efficient again: a robust standard error is an honest measurement of an imprecise estimate, not a more precise estimate. For efficiency you need weighted least squares.

Worked example — petrol consumption by state

PCONi = β1 + β2REGi + β3PRICEi + ui

where PCONi is petroleum consumption in the ith state in trillions of BTUs, REGi is motor vehicle registrations there in thousands, and PRICEi is the price of petrol in cents per gallon. Using data from 2005 across the 50 states:

PCON̂i =s.e.t = 4101 + 0.16 REGi(0.01)12.4 − 1885 PRICEi(750)−2.51 n = 50  ·  both slopes significant, and signed as theory predicts

The equation seems to have no problems at all. The coefficients are significant and in the hypothesised direction: more registered vehicles means more petrol burned, a higher price means less. A student who stopped here would have nothing obviously wrong to report.

Now run the Breusch–Pagan test. The auxiliary regression of the squared residuals on the two regressors is

û2 = 5,164,290 + 83.33 REGi − 2,475,027 PRICEi  ·  n = 50  ·  R2 = 0.197

with t-ratios of 3.32 and −1.68 on the two slopes. Testing H0: α2 = α3 = 0 gives LM = nR2 = 50 × 0.197 = 9.85 against a 5% critical χ2 value of 5.99 on two degrees of freedom. Reject: we have heteroscedasticity. The White test, whose auxiliary regression adds REG2, PRICE2 and the cross-product REG × PRICE, returns R2 = 0.85, so LM = 50 × 0.85 = 42.5 against a critical value of 11.07 on five degrees of freedom. Reject again, and emphatically.

Re-estimating the same model with heteroscedasticity-corrected standard errors:

PCON̂i =s.e.t = 4101 + 0.16 REGi(0.03)4.85 − 1885 PRICEi(1360)−1.39 n = 50  ·  price is now statistically insignificant

Every coefficient is unchanged, exactly as promised. Every standard error has grown — that on price by nearly a factor of two — and the conclusion about price has reversed. What looked like a significant negative price effect at the 5% level was an artefact of a standard error that was too small, and the honest report is that this sample cannot establish a price effect. Note how much rests on having run the test: nothing in the first set of results looked wrong.

A note on the printed figures The standard errors above are rounded to two decimal places, so recomputing the t-ratios from them will not reproduce the values shown — 0.16/0.01 is 16, not 12.4. The t-ratios are those the software reported from the unrounded output. This is worth knowing generally: a reported coefficient divided by a reported standard error rarely gives the reported t exactly, and the discrepancy is rounding, not error.

Remedy 3 — weighted least squares

Weighted least squares is a special type of generalised least squares, and in the presence of heteroscedasticity WLS is usually BLUE. Two cases are worth working through.

Case 1: σi2 is known. Here we derive a least squares estimator in which each observation is weighted by a factor proportional to the inverse of its error variance. Instead of estimating Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui, we divide everything through by σi:

Yi/σi = β1/σi + β2X2i/σi + β3X3i/σi + ui/σi

which we rewrite as Y* = β1* + β2*X2* + β3X3* + u*, the asterisks marking the transformed variables and parameters. The transformed disturbance is homoscedastic, and its variance is exactly 1:

var(u*) = var(ui/σi) = σi2/σi2 = 1

Case 2: h(X) is known. If σi2 = σ2h(X), then σi = σ√h(X), and dividing through by √h(X) instead achieves the same thing:

Yi/√h(X) = β1/√h(X) + β2X2i/√h(X) + β3X3i/√h(X) + ui/√h(X)

Again the transformed error is homoscedastic, but this time its variance is σ2 rather than 1, since var(ui/√h(X)) = σ2h(X)/h(X) = σ2.

What weighting is doing, and what it risks The use of weights implies that observations with a higher variance get a smaller weight in estimation. That is the whole idea: the noisy observations are told to count for less, which is precisely the information OLS throws away and precisely why WLS can beat it.
But what if we get h(X) wrong? Misspecification of the weighting function causes bias and inconsistency of the WLS estimator. That is a serious warning. OLS under heteroscedasticity is inefficient but unbiased; WLS with the wrong weights is neither. A remedy that can leave you worse off than the disease deserves to be applied only when you have a real reason to believe your h(X).

Remedy 4 — feasible generalised least squares

The previous case assumed we knew something about h(X). More often than not we do not know the exact form of the heteroscedasticity. The feasible, or estimated, generalised least squares estimator estimates h(X) instead of assuming it. One flexible approach specifies

var(u | X) = σ2 e(δ1 + δ2X2 + ⋯ + δkXk)

where X2 … Xk are the independent variables of the regression model and the δ's are unknown. The exponential is not decoration: it is what ensures the predicted variances are positive, which section 5 required of any h(X) and which a linear specification could not guarantee.

  1. Estimate Y = β1 + β2X2 + ⋯ + βkXk + u by OLS and obtain the residuals.
  2. Square the residuals, then log them, creating ln(û2). This is the step that makes the exponential form estimable — taking logs of the specification above turns a multiplicative model into one that is linear in the δ's, which is the trick of Unit 2A Part 2 applied to the variance rather than to the mean.
  3. Estimate the regression

    ln(û2) = α1 + δ2X2 + ⋯ + δkXk + e

    where the constant α1 differs from the δ1 above.
  4. Obtain the fitted values from that regression, Ẑi — the predicted values of ln(û2).
  5. Compute ĥi = eẐi, which is necessarily positive.
  6. Re-estimate the original equation using ĥ as the weights.
What FGLS costs Unlike the GLS estimator, FGLS is not BLUE. The weights are estimated rather than known, and that estimation has to be paid for. It is consistent and asymptotically efficient, and usually more efficient than OLS in the presence of heteroscedasticity — but those are large-sample properties. FGLS is mainly valid in large samples, and its testing procedures may only be valid in large samples too. In a sample of fifty, robust standard errors are the safer choice.

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

A model with three independent variables is estimated on 100 observations. The Breusch–Pagan auxiliary regression returns R2 = 0.128. Carry out the test at 5%, given that the critical χ2 values are 7.81 for three degrees of freedom and 16.92 for nine. Then say what would change if you ran a White test instead, and whether it would still reject.
Show solution

Breusch–Pagan. The auxiliary regression has the three original regressors on the right-hand side, so d = 3.

LM = nR2 = 100 × 0.128 = 12.8

Against χ2(3) = 7.81 in the right tail, 12.8 > 7.81, so we reject homoscedasticity at 5%.

Under White. The auxiliary regression would carry the three variables, their three squares and the three cross-products — 3 + 3 + 3(3 − 1)/2 = 9 terms — so the test would have nine degrees of freedom and a critical value of 16.92 instead of 7.81.

Would it still reject? That depends on the new R2, which we do not have, and the honest answer is that we cannot say. Adding six regressors cannot lower R2, so the new statistic is at least 12.8 — but it must now clear 16.92, which requires R2 to rise to at least 0.169. If the heteroscedasticity really is linear in the X's, the extra terms will add little and the White test may well fail to reject where Breusch–Pagan succeeded. That is the trade-off in one sentence: White can see shapes Breusch–Pagan cannot, and pays for the privilege in degrees of freedom whether or not those shapes are there.

Show that the general variance formula var(β̂2) = Σxi2σi2/(Σxi2)2 collapses to the familiar σ2/Σxi2 under homoscedasticity. Then explain why the conventional standard error is biased when the assumption fails, and say what determines the direction of the bias.
Show solution

The collapse. Under homoscedasticity every σi2 equals a common σ2, which is a constant and comes outside the summation:

Σxi2σ2/(Σxi2)2 = σ2Σxi2/(Σxi2)2 = σ2/Σxi2

So the familiar formula is not a different result but a special case of the general one.

Why the reported standard error is biased. The software computes √(σ̂2/Σxi2), where σ̂2 = RSS/(n − 2) estimates a single common variance. When there is no single common variance, that quantity is an average of the σi2, and an average put into the wrong formula. The correct formula weights each σi2 by xi2; the conventional one weights them all equally.

The direction. It follows from that difference in weighting. If the large variances sit on observations with large xi2 — that is, if σi2 is positively associated with distance from X̄ — the correct formula gives them more weight than the conventional one does, so the conventional standard error is too small. t-ratios are then too large and tests reject too often, which is the dangerous direction and the common one, since a variable whose variance grows with X has its noisiest observations out at the top of the range. Where the association is negative the conventional standard error is too large instead, and the test is merely conservative. There is no general answer, only that association.

A regression of household food expenditure on household income, estimated linearly on 800 households, produces a Breusch–Pagan statistic of 61.4 against a critical value of 3.84. A colleague proposes going straight to robust standard errors. Explain what you would do first and why, and what would tell you that the colleague's approach was in fact the right one.
Show solution

What to do first. Establish whether this is pure or impure heteroscedasticity, because the remedies are different and only one of them is correct. Robust standard errors treat the problem as pure — as a property of a correctly specified equation's disturbance. If the equation is in fact misspecified, robust standard errors will give an honest-looking measure of precision for a biased coefficient, which is a worse position than the one we started in, because it looks defensible.

Why suspicion is warranted here. An Engel curve is not linear. Food expenditure rises with income at a decreasing rate — that is Engel's law, and it has been known since 1857. A linear specification will therefore under-predict in the middle of the income range and over-predict at the ends, or the reverse, and those systematic residuals will grow with income and show up as heteroscedasticity. The obvious first move is a double-log or lin–log specification from Unit 2A Part 2, or a quadratic in income, which also has the merit of estimating an elasticity, which is what one actually wants from an Engel curve.

What would vindicate the colleague. If, after respecifying — logs, a quadratic term, any omitted variables such as household size — the Breusch–Pagan statistic remains large, then the heteroscedasticity has survived the specification search and is probably pure. That is exactly the situation robust standard errors exist for, and reason 3 of section 3 says to expect it here: as incomes grow, households have more discretionary income and more scope for choice, so the variance of food spending should genuinely rise with income even in a correctly specified model.

In the petrol example of section 9, the coefficient on price is −1885 both before and after the heteroscedasticity correction, but its t-ratio falls from −2.51 to −1.39. Explain why the coefficient did not move, why the standard error did, and what you would now report about the price effect.
Show solution

Why the coefficient did not move. Heteroscedasticity does not bias OLS, so there is nothing about the point estimate to correct. The formula β̂2 = Σxiyi/Σxi2 makes no reference to the error variance, so a different assumption about that variance cannot change the arithmetic. The robust procedure does not re-estimate the equation; it recomputes one quantity.

Why the standard error did. The conventional standard error is built from the wrong formula — it assumes a single common σ2. Here the variances of the observations differ, and the correct calculation weights each squared residual by xi2 rather than treating them alike. On this sample the correct calculation is nearly twice as large, so the conventional figure of 750 was understating the uncertainty by roughly half.

What to report. That the equation is heteroscedastic, on the evidence of both tests; that robust standard errors have been used; that registrations remain strongly significant with t = 4.85; and that the price effect is not statistically significant at 5%, since 1.39 falls short of roughly 2.01 for 47 degrees of freedom. The coefficient is still correctly signed and economically substantial, so the right conclusion is not that price does not matter but that this sample of fifty cannot establish that it does. It would be indefensible to report the original t of −2.51 having seen the tests.

Suppose σi2 = σ2h(Xi) with h(X) known. Show that dividing the regression through by √h(X) produces a homoscedastic error, and state its variance. Then say what happens if h(X) is specified wrongly, and why that makes the choice between WLS and robust standard errors a real one.
Show solution

The transformation. Dividing Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + ui through by √h(Xi) gives a new disturbance u* = ui/√h(Xi). Since dividing a random variable by a constant divides its variance by the square of that constant:

var(u*) = var(ui)/h(Xi) = σ2h(Xi)/h(Xi) = σ2

which does not depend on i. The transformed equation is homoscedastic and OLS applied to it is BLUE. Had we divided by σi itself instead, the same argument gives var(u*) = σi2/σi2 = 1.

If h(X) is wrong. The weights are then wrong, and misspecification of the weighting function causes bias and inconsistency of the WLS estimator. Note how much worse this is than the disease: OLS under heteroscedasticity is inefficient but unbiased and consistent, so it is merely imprecise. Badly weighted least squares is neither, and no amount of extra data will rescue it.

Why the choice is real. The two remedies trade different things. Robust standard errors make no assumption whatever about the form of the heteroscedasticity — they accept the inefficiency of OLS and simply measure it honestly. WLS promises efficiency, and can deliver BLUE, but only if you know h(X); guess it wrong and you have exchanged an unbiased imprecise estimator for a biased one. So the sensible rule is that WLS needs a genuine reason to believe a particular h(X) — a theoretical argument, or a known feature of the data such as group averages of known group sizes. Without such a reason, robust standard errors are the conservative and usually the correct choice.
