Endogeneity

When X is correlated with the error, OLS is biased and stays biased.

By the end of this unit you should be able to
  • state the exogeneity assumption in both its forms, and say what makes a regressor endogenous;
  • explain why endogeneity is the one assumption failure that a larger sample does not repair;
  • distinguish bias from inconsistency, and show that endogeneity delivers both;
  • name the three sources of endogeneity, and identify which one an equation suffers from;
  • write a simultaneous system in structural and in reduced form, and classify each variable as endogenous, exogenous or predetermined;
  • show algebraically that a jointly determined regressor is correlated with the disturbance;
  • derive plim(β̂2) for the Keynesian consumption function and sign the bias;
  • say why the reduced form may be estimated by OLS when the structural form may not.
Where this sits Unit 2 took the classical assumptions apart one at a time, and every failure it examined was survivable. Heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation cost efficiency and wreck the standard errors, but leave the coefficients themselves centred on the truth, so a large enough sample still tells you what you want to know. This unit is about the failure that is not like that. One case of it has already been met: Unit 2A Part 1's omitted variable bias. What follows gives the general condition, adds the source Unit 2 never touched — variables that determine each other — and shows why no amount of data helps. Unit 3C then supplies the repair.

1 · The assumption everything rests on

Return to the two-variable model, and to the assumption in Unit 1C's list that has done the most work:

Yi = β1 + β2Xi + ui

Unbiasedness of the OLS estimator required that the regressor carry no information about the disturbance. The assumption comes in two strengths, and both are worth having in mind:

E(ui | Xi) = 0   (the strong form)  ·  cov(Xi, ui) = 0   (the weak form)

The first says the disturbance averages to zero at every value of X, and it is what unbiasedness in a finite sample needs. The second says only that X and u are uncorrelated, and it is what consistency needs. The strong form implies the weak one; the weak one does not imply the strong. In this course the distinction rarely bites, and the shorthand for either is the same word.

Exogenous and endogenous A regressor is exogenous if it is uncorrelated with the disturbance: cov(Xi, ui) = 0. It is endogenous if it is not:

cov(Xi, ui) ≠ 0  ⟹  X is endogenous

The words are borrowed from simultaneous equation models, where endogenous means determined inside the system and exogenous means determined outside it. Section 4 returns to that reading. Used of a single equation, they simply record whether a regressor is correlated with the error.

Two things about the assumption are easy to lose sight of. First, it is required of every regressor, not just the one of interest. Second — and this is the reason endogeneity is not a local problem — one endogenous regressor contaminates the estimates of all the coefficients in the equation, including the coefficients on regressors that are perfectly well behaved. There is no such thing as a partly reliable regression.

A notation caution The slides write the weak form as E(u, X) = cov(u, X). Read that E as the expectation of the product, E(uX), which equals cov(u, X) only because E(u) = 0 is assumed separately. If the constant is in the equation that is guaranteed, so nothing goes wrong; but E(u, X) is not standard notation and it is worth writing E(uiXi) = 0 in an examination script.

2 · Why this failure is different

Unit 1C's proof of unbiasedness has one step where the assumption enters. Writing xi = Xi − X̄ and using the OLS weights ki = xi/Σxi2:

β̂2 = β2 + Σkiui = β2 + Σxiui / Σxi2

Everything then turned on the expectation of that second term. If X is fixed in repeated sampling, or at least uncorrelated with u, the term averages to zero and β̂2 is unbiased. If X moves with u it does not, and the estimator is biased by exactly the amount that the sampling error term fails to vanish.

So far this is the omitted variable story of Unit 2A Part 1. What is new is what happens next. Consider what a bigger sample does. Heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation both leave E(Σxiui) = 0, so the extra term shrinks as n grows and the estimator closes in on β2. Endogeneity does not: the numerator Σxiui/n converges on the covariance cov(X, u), which is not zero, and the estimator closes in on the wrong number.

Consistency, and its failure An estimator is consistent if its probability limit — its plim, the value it converges on as n grows without bound — equals the parameter it estimates. For a well-behaved regression,

plim(β̂2) = β2 + plim(Σxiui/n) / plim(Σxi2/n) = β2 + cov(X, u)/var(X)

because the two averages converge on the population covariance and the population variance. An estimator that converges on something else is inconsistent, and the gap cov(X, u)/var(X) is the asymptotic bias. Consistency is a weaker property than unbiasedness in one sense and a more basic one in another: an estimator can be biased in every finite sample and still consistent, which is tolerable, but an inconsistent estimator is answering a different question and more data will not change its answer.

Everything in Unit 2 was a problem about precision, and precision is bought with observations. Endogeneity is a problem about what the estimator is aiming at, and no number of observations improves the aim. A researcher with an endogenous regressor and a million observations has a very precise estimate of the wrong quantity, reported with a standard error small enough to make it look authoritative. That is the danger.

3 · Where endogeneity comes from

Three mechanisms produce a regressor correlated with the disturbance. The first has already been met, the second is named here and not pursued, and the third occupies the rest of this unit.

i Omitted variables. A variable that belongs in the equation and is left out of it goes into the disturbance instead. If it is correlated with a regressor that stayed in — and there would be no bias if it were not — that regressor is now correlated with the error. This is Unit 2A Part 1 exactly.
ii Measurement error. If the regressor is recorded with error, what appears in the data is X* = X + w, and the equation's disturbance absorbs −β2w. The recorded X* contains w, so regressor and disturbance move together by construction. The errors-in-variables problem is not examined this semester; the point to carry away is that it is an endogeneity problem, not a separate topic.
iii Simultaneity. Y and X determine each other, so a shock to Y feeds back into X. This is the case Unit 2 never touched, and the one economic theory produces most often.

The omitted variable case, once more

The standard illustration, and the one Unit 3C uses to introduce instruments, is the return to schooling. What a wage equation ought to contain is

ln(wagei) = β1 + β2Educi + β3Abili + εi

and ability is not observed. Unit 2A Part 1 offered a proxy; suppose no acceptable one exists. The equation that can actually be estimated is

ln(wagei) = β1 + β2Educi + ui   where   ui = β3Abili + εi

More able people are, on any plausible account, more educated, so cov(Educ, u) = β3cov(Educ, Abil) ≠ 0. Education is endogenous, and the sign of the bias follows the Unit 2A rule: with β3 > 0 and cov(Educ, Abil) > 0 the return to schooling is overstated, because part of what ability earns is being credited to the years of schooling that went with it.

4 · Simultaneous equations

Every model so far in this course has been a single equation with one variable on the left and the causation running one way:

Yi = β1 + β2X2i + β3X3i + … + βkXki + ui

Most relationships economists care about are not like that. Price determines quantity demanded and quantity demanded determines price; income determines consumption and consumption is most of income; money supply and interest rates move together. In each case the right-hand variable is determined jointly with the left-hand one, and the equation cannot be read as a one-way street.

The Keynesian aggregate demand model

The example the lecture works with is the smallest interesting one. The consumption function is a behavioural relationship:

Ct = β1 + β2Yt + ut

with C aggregate consumption, Y aggregate income and β2 the marginal propensity to consume. But in a closed economy with no government, income is not something handed down from outside — it is the sum of consumption and investment:

Yt = Ct + It

Those two equations are a simultaneous equation model, and they must be read together. The first is a behavioural equation, expressing a theory about how households act; the second is an identity, true by the definition of national income and carrying no parameters to estimate and no disturbance. Both are structural equations: together they portray the structure of the model, each endogenous variable written in terms of the others and of whatever comes from outside.

The three kinds of variable Endogenous variables are determined within the system. Here C and Y are both endogenous — solving the two equations together produces both of them at once.
Exogenous variables are determined outside it and taken as given. Here I is exogenous.
Predetermined variables are the exogenous variables plus lagged endogenous variables. Ct−1 was determined by last period's system, so as far as this period is concerned its value is already fixed, and it can be treated exactly as an exogenous variable can. The distinction matters in Unit 3C, where every predetermined variable in a system is a candidate instrument.
Which side of the equals sign proves nothing It is tempting to read off the endogenous variables as "the ones on the left". That is wrong, and the lecture makes the point with a deliberately abstract pair. In

Y = α1 + α2X + α3Z + u  ·  Z = β1 + β2Y + e

Z appears on the right of the first equation and is endogenous all the same, because the second equation determines it inside the system. X is exogenous. A variable is endogenous if the system determines it, wherever it happens to be written.

A supply and demand system

The other standard example is the market for a good. Demand depends on price, on income Y and on tastes T; supply depends on price and on tastes; and the market clears:

QiD = β1 + β2Pi + β3Yi + β4Ti + ui

QiS = α1 + α2Pi + α3Ti + ei

QiD = QiS

P and Q are jointly determined by the intersection of the two curves, so both are endogenous. Y and T are exogenous. And the question the unit is building towards is already visible here: if what we observe is a cloud of price–quantity pairs, each of them an intersection, what does a regression of Q on P actually estimate? Section 7 answers it exactly.

5 · The intuition: a shock that comes back

Before any algebra, the mechanism is worth walking through in words, because it is what an examination question usually asks for.

Take the consumption function and suppose the disturbance rises in some period — households feel confident, or a category of spending is mismeasured, or anything else that raises C without raising income first. Then:

1 ut rises, so Ct = β1 + β2Yt + ut rises.
2 But Yt = Ct + It, so income rises with it.
3 So the regressor Yt is high exactly when the disturbance ut is high: cov(Yt, ut) > 0. The assumption fails, and it fails in a known direction.

The same walk works in the market. A positive demand shock raises quantity demanded, which shifts the demand curve out along the supply curve, which raises the price. So cov(P, u) ≠ 0 in the demand equation, and by the mirror argument cov(P, e) ≠ 0 in the supply equation. Note what is not affected: income and tastes are determined outside the market, so they remain uncorrelated with both disturbances. Exogenous variables stay exogenous. That is what makes Unit 3C possible.

6 · The algebra: reduced form, and the size of the damage

The intuition can be made exact, and the route to it introduces the object Unit 3C is built on. Take the two structural equations and solve them for the endogenous variables in terms of the predetermined ones alone. Substituting the consumption function into the identity:

Yt = (β1 + β2Yt + ut) + It  ⟹  Yt(1 − β2) = β1 + It + ut

and therefore, with the companion expression for C obtained the same way,

Yt = β1/(1 − β2) + [1/(1 − β2)] It + [1/(1 − β2)] ut

Ct = β1/(1 − β2) + [β2/(1 − β2)] It + [1/(1 − β2)] ut

The reduced form The reduced form of a simultaneous system expresses each endogenous variable as a function of the predetermined variables only, plus a disturbance. Writing π1 = β1/(1 − β2), π2 = 1/(1 − β2), π3 = β1/(1 − β2), π4 = β2/(1 − β2), and vt = wt = ut/(1 − β2):

Yt = π1 + π2It + vt  ·  Ct = π3 + π4It + wt

The π's are the reduced form coefficients. Section 8 says what they mean and why they can be estimated when the β's cannot.

The covariance, exactly

The reduced form for Y makes the covariance a one-line calculation, because it puts the disturbance where it can be seen. Since E(ut) = 0,

cov(Yt, ut) = E(utYt) = E{ [β1/(1 − β2)]ut } + E{ [1/(1 − β2)]utIt } + E{ [1/(1 − β2)]ut2 }

The first term is zero because E(ut) = 0. The second is zero because investment is exogenous, so cov(ut, It) = 0. The third is not zero, because E(ut2) = var(ut) = σ2. Hence

cov(Yt, ut) = σ2 / (1 − β2)  > 0   for 0 < β2 < 1

which is the intuition of section 5 written as a number. Notice that it is bigger the closer the marginal propensity to consume is to one — the stronger the feedback, the worse the violation.

Bias, and then inconsistency

Estimating the consumption function by OLS gives, in deviation form with yt = Yt − Ȳ,

β̂2 = ΣytCt / Σyt2 = β2 + Σytut / Σyt2

For β̂2 to be unbiased we would need E(Ytut) = 0, and we have just shown it is σ2/(1 − β2). So the estimator is biased. Is it also inconsistent? Divide numerator and denominator by n, so that each becomes a sample moment, and take probability limits:

plim(β̂2) = β2 + plim(Σytut/n) / plim(Σyt2/n)

As n grows, the sample covariance between Y and u converges on the population covariance just computed, and the sample variance of Y converges on σY2. Therefore

plim(β̂2) = β2 + [σ2/(1 − β2)] / σY2 = β2 + [1/(1 − β2)](σ2/σY2)

The result, and how to state it With 0 < β2 < 1 and both variances positive, the second term is strictly positive. So plim(β̂2) > β2: OLS overestimates the marginal propensity to consume, and goes on overestimating it however large the sample gets. The estimator is biased in every finite sample and inconsistent in the limit — the bias does not disappear, which is precisely the property that makes endogeneity worth a unit of its own.

One further step is worth taking, because it turns the result into something a figure can be checked against. The reduced form says Yt = π1 + π2It + ut/(1 − β2), so var(Y) = [var(I) + σ2]/(1 − β2)2. Substituting that for σY2 above and cancelling:

plim(β̂2) = β2 + (1 − β2) · σ2/(σ2 + σI2)

Read that as a sentence. The asymptotic bias is the distance from β2 to one, multiplied by the share of income's variation that comes from the disturbance rather than from investment. Two limiting cases make it concrete. If investment is the only thing that moves, σI2 dominates, the share goes to zero and OLS is almost fine. If investment never moves, income varies only because u varies, the share is one, and plim(β̂2) = 1 whatever the true propensity to consume is — the regression simply recovers the accounting identity. The size of the simultaneity bias is governed by how much of the regressor's variation is the feedback and how much is genuinely from outside.

7 · What a regression of Q on P actually estimates

The market model of section 4 admits the same treatment, and the answer is exact and worth knowing. Strip it to two curves and their shocks:

demand: Qi = a1 + a2Pi + ui  ·  supply: Qi = b1 + b2Pi + ei

with a2 < 0 and b2 > 0. Setting the two equal and solving gives the reduced form for price, Pi = (a1 − b1 + ui − ei)/(b2 − a2), and working through the covariance and the variance as before produces

plim(β̂) = (1 − w) b2 + w a2   where   w = σe2 / (σu2 + σe2)

OLS estimates a blend of the two curves The slope OLS converges on is a weighted average of the supply slope and the demand slope, with weights given by where the shocks come from. If only demand shifts (w = 0), the equilibria trace out the supply curve and OLS estimates b2 — the supply slope, whatever equation you thought you were fitting. If only supply shifts (w = 1), OLS estimates the demand slope a2. If both shift, it estimates neither, and the number it reports need not even have the right sign.

This is the classic identification problem, and stating it this way makes the way out visible. The scatter is informative about supply precisely when something is moving demand and nothing is moving supply. If we could observe that demand shifter — income, say, which appears in the demand equation of section 4 and not in the supply equation — we could use the variation in price that it causes, and discard the rest. That is exactly what an instrument is, and it is Unit 3C's subject.

8 · The reduced form, and what it is good for

Section 6 produced the reduced form as a step in an algebraic argument. It is more than that. Look again at what it contains: on the right-hand side of every reduced form equation there are only predetermined variables. Investment is exogenous, so it is uncorrelated with u, and therefore with v = u/(1 − β2) as well.

Why the reduced form is estimable when the structural form is not The classical assumption that fails in the structural equation holds in the reduced form. Every regressor there is predetermined, so cov(regressor, disturbance) = 0, and OLS applied to a reduced form equation is unbiased and consistent in the ordinary way.
This is the hinge of Unit 3C: if the structural parameters can be recovered from the reduced form coefficients, they can be estimated after all, by estimating the reduced form first. That recovery is not always possible, and whether it is is the identification problem.

The reduced form coefficients have an economic reading of their own. π2 = 1/(1 − β2) is the derivative of income with respect to investment: it says by how much income changes in the same period when investment changes by one unit. Coefficients of this kind are called impact multipliers, and π2 is the Keynesian investment multiplier that appears in a first-year macroeconomics course. A reduced form estimate of π2 = 5 says a dollar of extra investment raises income by five dollars on impact — and, since π2 = 1/(1 − β2), that the marginal propensity to consume is 0.8.

That last inference is the whole of Unit 3C in miniature: a structural parameter, recovered from a reduced form coefficient that OLS was entitled to estimate. Whether the trick can always be worked, what to do when there is more than one way to work it, and how to handle systems too large to solve by hand, are the questions the next unit takes up.

9 · Tutorial

Draft questions, to be replaced by the tutorial sheet issued in class.

Explain carefully the difference between a biased estimator and an inconsistent one. Give an example of an estimator that is biased but consistent, and say why endogeneity produces an estimator that is both. Why does the second property make endogeneity a more serious problem than heteroscedasticity?
Show solution

Bias is a property of a fixed sample size: an estimator is unbiased if E(β̂) = β, that is, if it is right on average over repeated samples of size n. Consistency is a property of the limit: an estimator is consistent if plim(β̂) = β, that is, if it converges on the true value as n grows without bound.

Biased but consistent. Unit 1E's σ̃2 = Σû2/n is biased downward in every finite sample, because it does not charge for the degrees of freedom used to estimate the coefficients. But the bias is of order 1/n, so it vanishes: σ̃2 is consistent. Such an estimator is usable — a large enough sample makes the bias negligible.

Endogeneity gives both. β̂2 = β2 + Σxiui/Σxi2. The expectation of the second term is non-zero, so the estimator is biased. Dividing top and bottom by n, that term converges on cov(X, u)/var(X), which is also non-zero, so the estimator is inconsistent as well. The bias does not shrink with n; it settles at a constant.

Why it is worse. Heteroscedasticity leaves OLS unbiased and consistent; what it damages is efficiency and the standard errors, and both of those can be repaired — by a robust standard error, by WLS, or simply by collecting more data. Endogeneity damages the estimate itself, and collecting more data makes matters worse in one practical sense: the standard error shrinks around the wrong number, so the estimate looks more trustworthy the more of it there is.

A researcher estimates a wage equation on 40,000 observations and reports a return to schooling of 9.2% per year with a standard error of 0.3 percentage points, concluding that "the effect is precisely estimated and the sample is large enough that any bias is negligible." Criticise the conclusion.
Show solution

The conclusion confuses precision with accuracy, and it invokes the sample size for a property the sample size cannot deliver.

The standard error measures the wrong thing. A standard error of 0.3 points says the estimate would vary little across repeated samples of 40,000. It says nothing whatever about whether those samples are all centred on the truth. If ability is omitted and correlated with schooling, every one of them is centred on β2 + cov(Educ, u)/var(Educ), and the small standard error simply means that wrong centre is pinned down tightly.

"Large enough that any bias is negligible" is the specific error. That reasoning is correct for a bias of order 1/n — the σ̃2 case. It is exactly wrong for an endogeneity bias, whose plim is a constant independent of n. Forty thousand observations remove sampling noise and leave the asymptotic bias untouched.

The likely direction. If ability raises wages and is positively correlated with schooling, both factors are positive and 9.2% is an overestimate of the causal return. The honest report says so and either finds an instrument (Unit 3C) or presents the number as a conditional association rather than a return.

For the system Ct = β1 + β2Yt + ut, Yt = Ct + It + Gt, with government spending G exogenous: (a) classify every variable; (b) derive the reduced form for Y and for C; (c) obtain cov(Yt, ut); (d) if β2 = 0.75, σ2 = 4 and the exogenous spending I + G has variance 36, compute the probability limit of the OLS estimate of β2.
Show solution

(a) Classification. C and Y are endogenous — the two equations determine them jointly. I and G are exogenous, hence predetermined. There are two equations and two endogenous variables, as there must be.

(b) Reduced form. Substitute the consumption function into the identity and write At = It + Gt for autonomous spending:

Yt(1 − β2) = β1 + At + ut

Yt = β1/(1 − β2) + [1/(1 − β2)]At + [1/(1 − β2)]ut

Ct = Yt − At = β1/(1 − β2) + [β2/(1 − β2)]At + [1/(1 − β2)]ut

Both are functions of predetermined variables only, which is the test that the reduced form has been reached.

(c) The covariance. From the reduced form for Y, and using E(u) = 0, cov(u, A) = 0 and E(u2) = σ2:

cov(Yt, ut) = E(utYt) = σ2/(1 − β2)

Positive, so Y is endogenous and OLS on the consumption function is biased upward.

(d) The probability limit. Use the form derived in section 6, with the exogenous variance in place of σI2:

plim(β̂2) = β2 + (1 − β2)σ2/(σ2 + σA2) = 0.75 + 0.25 × 4/40 = 0.775

An asymptotic bias of 0.025, or about 3.3% of the true value. Checking it the long way gives the same answer: cov(Y, u) = 4/0.25 = 16, var(Y) = (36 + 4)/0.252 = 640, and 0.75 + 16/640 = 0.775.

In the market model Q = a1 + a2P + u (demand) and Q = b1 + b2P + e (supply), a researcher regresses quantity on price over 30 years of data for a staple food and obtains a slope of +0.6, which he reports as a supply elasticity. (a) What is OLS actually converging on? (b) Under what circumstance would his interpretation be defensible? (c) He then repeats the exercise for a luxury good and obtains −1.2. Has anything about the method changed?
Show solution

(a) What OLS converges on. Neither structural slope, but the blend of section 7:

plim(β̂) = (1 − w)b2 + w a2,   w = σe2/(σu2 + σe2)

Both curves are being hit by shocks, and the observed scatter is the cloud of intersections. The number 0.6 is a variance-weighted average of a positive supply slope and a negative demand slope, and there is no way to tell from the regression which weights produced it.

(b) When the interpretation would be defensible. Only if w ≈ 0 — if supply is essentially stable and virtually all the movement comes from demand. Then the equilibria are being dragged along a fixed supply curve and the scatter does trace it out. For an agricultural staple that is the wrong way round: harvests are the volatile side and demand is steady, which puts w near one and makes the estimate closer to the demand slope. His positive number is therefore hard to square with the story he tells about it.

(c) Nothing has changed. The sign flip says only that the mix of shocks differs between the two markets. It is evidence about w, not about elasticities. Neither regression identifies either curve, and the fact that one produced a plausible supply number and the other a plausible demand number is precisely the trap: a simultaneity-biased estimate can land anywhere between the two slopes, including on a value that looks exactly like the answer expected.

10 · Check yourself

A regressor is endogenous. Compared with heteroscedasticity, the distinctive damage is that
Correct — plim(β̂₂) = β₂ + cov(X,u)/var(X), a constant that does not shrink with n.
Heteroscedasticity also wrecks the standard errors and costs efficiency, yet leaves OLS unbiased and consistent. What is peculiar to endogeneity is that the estimator converges on the wrong number, so more data does not help. R² is not the issue at all.
In C = β₁ + β₂Y + u with Y = C + I, why is income correlated with the disturbance?
Correct — the feedback runs u → C → Y, so income is high exactly when the disturbance is high: cov(Y, u) = σ²/(1 − β₂) > 0.
The correlation is created by the identity, not by measurement or by lags. A shock to consumption raises consumption, and because Y = C + I it raises income too, so the regressor moves with the error by construction. Investment's volatility affects the size of the bias, not its existence.
The reduced form of a simultaneous system expresses each endogenous variable in terms of
Correct — and because every regressor is then predetermined, OLS on a reduced form equation is consistent.
The defining feature is that no endogenous variable survives on the right-hand side: everything there is exogenous or a lagged endogenous variable. That is what makes the reduced form estimable by OLS when the structural equations are not. Lagged endogenous variables are predetermined, but they are not the only ones allowed.
Quantity is regressed on price using market equilibria. If only supply is shifting, OLS converges on
Correct — plim(β̂) = (1 − w)b₂ + wa₂ with w = 1, so the estimate is a₂. A moving curve traces out the one that stays still.
The curve that moves sweeps its intersections along the curve that stays put, so shifting supply reveals demand. The weighted average is the general case; here the weight w = σ²ₑ/(σ²ᵤ + σ²ₑ) equals one, which picks out a₂ exactly.

The examinable core: the exogeneity assumption in both forms, the three sources of endogeneity, the difference between bias and inconsistency, deriving the reduced form of a two-equation system, showing cov(Y, u) = σ2/(1 − β2), and signing the resulting bias. The derivation in section 6 is the one that appears on examination papers most often, and it is worth being able to write out from the structural equations without notes.
